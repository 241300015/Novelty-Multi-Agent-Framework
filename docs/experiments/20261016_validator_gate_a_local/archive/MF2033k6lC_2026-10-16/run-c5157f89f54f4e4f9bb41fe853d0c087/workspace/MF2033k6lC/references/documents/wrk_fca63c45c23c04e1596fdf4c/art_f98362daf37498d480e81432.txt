Abstract

Graph Neural Additive Networks (GNANs) have emerged as an effective, interpretable-by-design graph architecture for learning on graphs. They achieve model transparency by combining per-feature shape functions with a simple distance-based reweighting. Despite the effectiveness of GNAN, we identify the following limitations:
i) node-wise relevance scores are computed as a weighted aggregation over all graph nodes, allowing irrelevant nodes to inherit spurious relevance from relevant ones;
ii) graph topology is encoded only through shortest-path distances, and structurally distinct graphs may have identical distances;
iii) feature and distance information are combined multiplicatively, thus entangling feature, distance, and interaction effects into a single, rescalable term.
To address these limitations, we introduce 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA}, which represents topology through a vocabulary of mined motifs and decomposes the contribution of each node into three additive channels: a feature channel, a structural channel, and a feature–structure interaction channel. We center these channels around a training-data baseline that gives every graph equal weight, regardless of its size, making the decomposition well defined and unique.
Across ten graph classification and regression benchmarks, 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} outperforms GNAN on most datasets and narrows the gap to standard black-box GNNs. Because every channel is centered and attached to a single node, the explanations of 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} stay local to the node that generates them and reconstruct the model output exactly while providing interpretability at different levels of granularity. 1 Introduction

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for learning on graphs (Scarselli et al., 2008; Bacciu et al., 2020), with applications in high-stakes scenarios like power grid analysis (Varbella et al., 2024), health forecasting (Hu et al., 2024), software vulnerability detection (Lekeufack Foulefack and Marchetto, 2024), and drug discovery (Wong et al., 2024).
However, the lack of transparency in black-box GNNs has motivated interpretable architectures that trade predictive performance for model transparency (Rudin, 2019).

The prominent examples of these interpretable-by-design architectures are Graph Neural Additive Networks (GNANs) (Bechler-Speicher et al., 2024), which extend Generalized Additive Models (Hastie and Tibshirani, 1986) to graphs.
They learn one univariate function per feature, and linearly combine these over all nodes, scaling the contribution of each node by a learnable, univariate distance function.
This composition of simple, univariate functions gives users full knowledge of how the model aggregated information, and of which features are mostly relevant, enabling model-level understanding and debugging. GNANs have been further extended to temporal, heterogeneous data (Bechler-Speicher et al., 2025), to incorporate parameter-efficient transformations (Siddartha Reddy et al., 2025), and to Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation for LLMs (Sade et al., 2026).

Despite the effectiveness of GNANs, in this work we identify the following three limitations that can hinder both their downstream performance and their interpretability:
i) the node-wise importance scores extracted by GNAN for node ii do not represent its own contribution, but are a blurred aggregation of the importances of every node; ii) GNAN relies exclusively on shortest-path distances to encode graph structure, potentially missing other informative topological properties.
iii) GNAN multiplies the univariate feature function with the shortest-path distance reweighting, resulting in a less interpretable mixture of feature, distance, and interaction effects.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce the Attribute-Motif Interaction Graph Additive model (𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA}).
𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} relies on motifs automatically discovered from the training set, with each node contributing based on its features and on which motifs it appears in.
The model linearly combines three additive channels – feature, structural, and feature–structure interaction – each centered under a graph-balanced reference distribution that assigns equal mass to each training graph, ensuring the three channels form a unique canonical decomposition.
Summarizing, our main contributions are:

•

We identify three limitations of current graph additive models: node-wise importance scores do not isolate node-local contributions, shortest-path distances miss richer topology, and multiplicative feature–distance coupling obscures the separate feature, distance, and interaction effects.

•

We introduce 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA}, an interpretable-by-design GNN that replaces distance-based aggregation with automatically mined motifs, enabling the model to capture richer structural patterns while providing disentangled feature- and motif-based contributions.

•

We develop a graph-balanced centering scheme that makes the additive decomposition well-defined and unique, allowing predictions to be decomposed into separate attribute, structural, and attribute–structure interaction contributions.

Across ten graph classification and regression benchmarks, 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} outperforms GNAN on eight datasets and G-NAMRFF on nine.
Furthermore, in a controlled experiment, 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} successfully recovers the known task composition from label supervision alone, and achieves an explanation accuracy of 0.990.99 and 1.001.00 of AUCROC score on two synthetic datasets with known ground-truth explanations.
Although black-box GNNs remain stronger on most real-world benchmarks, 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} narrows the gap while providing an exact, interpretable decomposition of every prediction.

2 Related works

Interpretable Graph Models.
Interpretable-by-design GNNs were proposed to overcome the limitations of traditional post-hoc explainers (Rudin, 2019; Longa et al., 2025) by making the explanation part of the model’s computation.
Examples include prototype-based GNNs – predicting from the similarity between the input and learned prototypes (Zhang et al., 2022; Ragno et al., 2024) – and self-explainable GNNs – where a detector extracts a compact subgraph used by a classifier (Miao et al., 2022; Azzolin et al., 2025).
Nonetheless, both families were shown to be potentially unfaithful (Christiansen et al., 2023; Azzolin et al., 2026). Recently, Ragno et al. (2025) proposed to build GNN layers expressing the model’s decisions as logical rules. This construction is, however, limited to classification tasks, and for complex tasks it may yield long and deeply nested formulas, hindering human understanding (Miller, 1956).
We focus on graph neural additive models (Bechler-Speicher et al., 2024) because their visual explanations have the potential to be more easily interpretable.

Motif- and Subgraph-Based Graph Representations.
Recurring subgraphs, or motifs, capture higher-order structure in complex networks (Milo et al., 2002). Standard message-passing GNNs are bounded by the 1-WL test and can therefore fail to distinguish graphs that differ in simple substructures, such as triangles (Xu et al., 2018; Morris et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020). Subgraph- and motif-aware methods address this limitation by augmenting nodes with local substructure counts (Bouritsas et al., 2023), processing collections of subgraphs (Bevilacqua et al., 2022), or constructing motif vocabularies for richer node and graph representations (Yu and Gao, 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025).
Similarly to previous work integrating motif vocabularies, 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} achieves this whilst keeping the feature and interaction effects separate, enabling model transparency by design.

Both GNAN and G-NAMRFF share these limitations:

Node-wise relevance scores are blurred.
GNAN computes the relevance score of node ii by aggregating feature contributions from all nodes jj, weighted by their distance from ii, see Equation 2.
Consequently, this score does not represent node ii’s own feature contribution, but in conjunction with every other node in the graph.
For example, a node with zero feature contribution on its own can receive a non-zero relevance score just because it is close to a node with a positive score.
Figure 1(a) illustrates this case: node uu carries positive contribution ∑kfk​(xu​k)=1\sum_{k}f_{k}(x_{uk})=1, whilst node vv carries none ∑kfk​(xv​k)=0\sum_{k}f_{k}(x_{vk})=0.
With a distance weighting ρ\rho of one from a node to itself and 0.50.5 between uu and vv, Equation 2 gives RuGNAN=1R_{u}^{\mathrm{GNAN}}=1 and RvGNAN=0.5R_{v}^{\mathrm{GNAN}}=0.5,
even though the only nonzero feature contribution originates from uu. G-NAMRFF faces a similar issue, as it mixes neighboring features within its finite receptive field before assigning relevance scores.

GNAN and G-NAMRFF can miss relevant topological information.

GNAN represents topology through shortest-path distance.
This can identify how far nodes lie from one another, but it does not determine which subgraphs connect them.
For instance, Figure 1(b) shows an example of the graph K3,3K_{3,3} and the triangular prism, where both have six nodes with the same distance profile from every node.
Hence, Equation 1 scores them identically for every ρ\rho and {fk}k=1d\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}, even though only the prism contains a triangle.
Laplacian filtering does not resolve this limitation in general: with constant node features, both graphs are 3-regular, so L~G​𝟏=0\widetilde{L}_{G}\mathbf{1}=0 and G-NAMRFF also maps them identically.

Features, topology, and interaction effects are mixed.

GNAN combines the feature and distance function multiplicatively.
This product, however, does not separate the effect of features from that of topological information, and how these interact.
Consider the following example, with f⁡(x)=3+xf(x)=3+x and ρ⁡(u)=2+u\rho(u)=2+u.
Then, ρ⁡(u)​f​(x)\rho(u)f(x) yields 6+2​x+3​u+x​u6+2x+3u+xu which is composed of a constant term, a feature-only term, a distance-only term, and a joint term.
Crucially, all these terms are implicit in the multiplication.
In addition, the individual factors also lack a fixed scale, such that for any nonzero γ\gamma,
ρ⁡(u)​f​(x)=[γ​f​(x)]​[ρ⁡(u)/γ].\rho(u)f(x)=\bigl[\gamma f(x)\bigr]\bigl[\rho(u)/\gamma\bigr].
This implies that, although the two univariate functions and hence the explanations change, the prediction remains the same.
Interpreting their absolute magnitudes is, therefore, difficult and requires an explicit reference convention.
G-NAMRFF replaces the GNAN product with gk​((HG​X)i​k)g_{k}((H_{G}X)_{ik}), but features and topology have already been mixed inside its argument, and therefore the same argument above applies.

4.2 Feature, structural, and interaction channels

Each channel is built from centered univariate shape functions, defined precisely in Section 4.3:

F⁡(x)=∑k=1dfk​(xk),S⁡(c)=∑m=1Msm​(cm),A⁡(x)=∑k=1dak​(xk),B⁡(c)=∑m=1Mbm​(cm).F(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}f_{k}(x_{k}),\quad S(c)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}s_{m}(c_{m}),\quad A(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}(x_{k}),\quad B(c)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}b_{m}(c_{m}).

The interaction therefore expands as I⁡(x,c):=A⁡(x)​B​(c)=∑k=1d∑m=1Mak​(xk)​bm​(cm)I(x,c):=A(x)B(c)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{m=1}^{M}a_{k}(x_{k})b_{m}(c_{m}). Accordingly, the node-level interaction contribution is Iv:=I⁡(xv,cv)I_{v}:=I(x_{v},c_{v}). Every interaction term involves one feature and one motif. The model learns d+Md+M interaction shapes, while exposing all d​MdM feature–motif pairs. Although the d​MdM pairwise functions could instead be learned directly, we favor the factorized form since humans can only track a few items at once (Cowan, 2001). This keeps the number of independently inspectable functions traceable as dd and MM grow.
The uncentered functions f~k\widetilde{f}_{k}, s~m\widetilde{s}_{m}, a~k\widetilde{a}_{k}, and b~m\widetilde{b}_{m} are parameterized by small univariate MLPs for numerical features and motif coordinates. For categorical features, f~k\widetilde{f}_{k} and a~k\widetilde{a}_{k} are scalar lookup tables with one learned value per category; when a categorical variable is supplied as a one-hot vector, the full group is treated as a single variable. The centered shape functions used above are obtained from these uncentered functions as described in Section 4.3, and each can be inspected directly.

4.3 Graph-balanced canonicalization

Equation 3 separates the node score into feature, structural, and interaction channels. This decomposition is not identifiable because constants can be shifted between the channels and the intercept without changing the represented function. We remove this ambiguity by centering the channels. Centering, however, requires choosing a reference distribution. A simple average over all training nodes would give larger graphs greater weight. 4.4 Factorized Motif Explanations

Factorized Motif Explanations (FME) read the quantities already computed
by the model. No surrogate model or post-hoc approximation is required.

The three graph-level channel totals are

which reconstruct the score, s⁡(G)=β0+ΦF​(G)+ΦS​(G)+ΦI​(G)s(G)=\beta_{0}+\Phi^{F}(G)+\Phi^{S}(G)+\Phi^{I}(G).
At node level, FME reports the three terms F⁡(xv)F(x_{v}), S⁡(cv)S(c_{v}),
A⁡(xv)​B​(cv)A(x_{v})B(c_{v}), or their sum z⁡(xv,cv)z(x_{v},c_{v}).

Motif and feature–motif explanations.
The contribution of motif type mm to the structural channel, and of
feature–motif pair (k,m)(k,m) to the interaction, are

QmS​(G)=∑v∈Vsm​(cv​m),Qk​mI​(G)=∑v∈Vak​(xv​k)​bm​(cv​m)Q^{S}_{m}(G)=\sum_{v\in V}s_{m}(c_{vm}),\qquad Q^{I}_{km}(G)=\sum_{v\in V}a_{k}(x_{vk})b_{m}(c_{vm})

with ΦS​(G)=∑m=1MQmS​(G)\Phi^{S}(G)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}Q^{S}_{m}(G) and
ΦI​(G)=∑k=1d∑m=1MQk​mI​(G)\Phi^{I}(G)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Q^{I}_{km}(G). The first identity
ranks reusable motif templates; the second produces a d×Md\times M matrix
of feature–motif contributions.

Exactness and occurrence explanations.
The identities above exactly reconstruct the graph score and the structural and interaction channels. For each node and motif type, FME computes the change in the structural and interaction contributions of the node when motif participation moves from zero to the observed level. It then distributes this change equally across all occurrences of the motif that contain the node. Adding the zero-participation baseline terms recovers the original motif contributions. These allocations remain valid when motif occurrences overlap and are equivariant to vertex relabeling. Their definitions and verification are given in Appendix A.2. They describe the model computation and should not be interpreted as the causal effects of deleting a motif.

5 Experimental Evaluation

We evaluate 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} along three axes: whether it recovers known feature, structural, and interaction effects; whether its explanations identify
ground-truth predictive patterns; and whether its transparency preserves
useful predictive performance.

Experimental Protocol.
We evaluate four graph-classification datasets
(Mutagenicity, NCI1, PROTEINS, and PTC-MR) and six graph-regression datasets
(BA-Reg 1, BA-Reg 2, Crippen, QM9-α\alpha, QM9-HOMO, and QM9-μ\mu).
We compare 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} with standard, widely used black-box GNNs like GCN (Kipf and Welling, 2016), GIN (Xu et al., 2018), and Graph Transformer (Shi et al., 2020), and with the interpretable GNAN and G-NAMRFF baselines under the same splits and preprocessing.
For each seed, motif discovery, normalization, and centering use only the corresponding training split. Checkpoints are selected on validation performance. Results
are mean ±\pm standard deviation over five seeds, using AUROC for
classification and MAE for regression.
Dataset statistics and preprocessing are reported in Appendix B.1; optimization, hyperparameter selection, and training details are given in Appendix B.3.

6 From Global Rules to a Local Explanation

Next, we showcase an example of how 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} provides explanations for the Mutagenicity dataset. Explanations are read directly from the model, without a surrogate or post-hoc approximation.
Because 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} channels are centered using graph-balanced training marginals, zero defines a common reference that gives every training graph equal weight, regardless of its size; positive and negative contributions respectively raise and lower the model score relative to this reference.

Global Decision Rules.
Figure 4 reveals a sparse, context-dependent decision rule. Most nonzero feature and structural effects are concentrated in a small set of atom types and motifs. For motif m3m_{3}, the structural effect s3​(k)s_{3}(k) increases with node participation kk. Its overall motif-related contribution, however, also depends on the participating atom, because the interaction I3​(atom,k)I_{3}(\mathrm{atom},k) changes both magnitude and sign across atom types. As participation increases, the interaction becomes positive for fluorine and negative for iodine, bromine, and sulfur. The same motif can therefore raise or lower the model score depending on the atom in which it occurs. The feature-only and structure-only channels capture the corresponding main effects, while the interaction channel additionally captures how the effect of motif participation varies across atom types.

Local Explanations.
Figure 5 contextualizes the global rule above over a molecule. Each row decomposes an atom contribution into feature, structural, and interaction effects. The nitrogen node v1v_{1} contributes −0.40-0.40 through its atom type. The dominant repeated signal is the bromine m3m_{3} interaction identified above: it contributes −0.64-0.64 to
each bromine node v2v_{2}, v3v_{3}, and v4v_{4}, driving each node total to
−0.87-0.87. At graph level, the feature channel contributes ΦF​(G)=−0.70\Phi^{F}(G)=-0.70, while
the structural contributions largely cancel, yielding ΦS​(G)=0.12\Phi^{S}(G)=0.12. The interaction
channel dominates with ΦI​(G)=−2.25\Phi^{I}(G)=-2.25. Hence
∑vzv=−2.83\sum_{v}z_{v}=-2.83, and adding the bias term β0=0.67\beta_{0}=0.67 gives the exact graph score s⁡(G)=−2.16s(G)=-2.16.
To conclude, this example shows how to contextualize the global decision rule above into a local interpretation of each node’s relevance, for a given molecular graph.

7 Conclusion

We introduced 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA}, an interpretable-by-design graph model that replaces distance-only summaries with mined motifs. Graph-balanced centering yields canonical feature, structural, and interaction channels, while Factorized Motif Explanations reconstruct predictions exactly from graph to feature–motif level. Across ten benchmarks, 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} outperformed GNAN and G-NAMRFF on eight and nine datasets, respectively. It also recovered known generative effects from graph-level supervision and identified ground-truth explanatory nodes on two synthetic tasks. These results show that richer structural modeling can coexist with exact model transparency.

Limitations. These results open several directions for extending 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA}. Enriching the motif vocabulary and exploring more expressive, explicitly decomposable interactions may help close the remaining accuracy gap with black-box GNNs on several real-world benchmarks. Scalable counting algorithms or sampling-based motif estimates could extend the vocabulary beyond the small induced graphlets supported by the current exact enumeration procedure. Finally, Factorized Motif Explanations provide a faithful decomposition of the learned score and a starting point for formulating hypotheses about motif effects. Testing these hypotheses through controlled graph interventions, with explicit causal assumptions, is a promising direction toward causal analysis; the current allocations themselves do not estimate causal effects.

A.3 Sparsity Penalty and Reciprocal-Scale Balancing

The sparsity penalty used in the training objective is

Ω=∑k=1d𝔼Xgb​[|fk​(Xk)|+|ak​(Xk)|]+∑m=1M𝔼Cgb​[|sm​(Cm)|+|bm​(Cm)|].\Omega=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{gb}}_{X}\left[|f_{k}(X_{k})|+|a_{k}(X_{k})|\right]+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{gb}}_{C}\left[|s_{m}(C_{m})|+|b_{m}(C_{m})|\right].

(7)

The terms involving fkf_{k} and sms_{m} promote sparse main-effect
explanations. The terms involving aka_{k} and bmb_{m} also select a magnitude
within the reciprocal-rescaling class of the factorized interaction.

Define

RA=∑k=1d𝔼Xgb​|ak​(Xk)|,RB=∑m=1M𝔼Cgb​|bm​(Cm)|.R_{A}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{gb}}_{X}|a_{k}(X_{k})|,\qquad R_{B}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{gb}}_{C}|b_{m}(C_{m})|.

Under ak↦γ​aka_{k}\mapsto\gamma a_{k} and
bm↦bm/γb_{m}\mapsto b_{m}/\gamma, the interaction remains unchanged, while its
contribution to the penalty becomes

RI​(γ)=|γ|​RA+RB|γ|.R_{I}(\gamma)=|\gamma|R_{A}+\frac{R_{B}}{|\gamma|}.

For RA,RB>0R_{A},R_{B}>0, the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality gives

RI​(γ)≥2​RA​RB,R_{I}(\gamma)\geq 2\sqrt{R_{A}R_{B}},

with equality when

|γ|=RBRA.|\gamma|=\sqrt{\frac{R_{B}}{R_{A}}}.

Thus, among equivalent factorizations, the penalty selects the magnitude
that equalizes the two penalized factors. The global sign remains
unidentified, but it cancels in every product
ak​(xk)​bm​(cm)a_{k}(x_{k})b_{m}(c_{m}) and therefore does not affect any reported
feature–motif contribution. If either RAR_{A} or RBR_{B} is zero, the
interaction vanishes almost everywhere under the reference distribution,
and its scale is immaterial.

B.1 Datasets and Preprocessing

We use Mutagenicity, NCI1, PROTEINS, and PTC-MR from
TUDataset Morris et al. (2020) for binary classification, loaded
with PyTorch Geometric 2.7.0. Regression benchmarks comprise BA-Reg 1,
BA-Reg 2, and Crippen from Zhang et al. (2024), together with the
μ\mu, α\alpha, and HOMO targets from QM9 Ramakrishnan et al. (2014).

We remove self-loops and represent each input as a simple undirected graph.

mapped through a learned lookup table. Numerical features are standardized
coordinate-wise using training-node statistics, with unit scale for
constant coordinates. Regression targets are standardized on the training
graphs and returned to their original units before computing MAE. All
statistics, motif discovery, and motif normalization are fitted on the
training split and then frozen.

We use a 70/15/15 train/validation/test split for each of five seeds, {11,22,33,44,55}\{11,22,33,44,55\}. Each seed determines both the data split and model training. The three QM9 tasks share the same graph indices within each seed. Reported results are averaged over these five seeds, with preprocessing fitted independently on each training split.

B.2 Motif Discovery Protocol

For a candidate graphlet PP, define

𝒪P(G)={U⊆V:|U|=q,G[U]≃P},NP(G)=|𝒪P(G)|,\mathcal{O}_{P}(G)=\{U\subseteq V:|U|=q,\;G[U]\simeq P\},\qquad N_{P}(G)=|\mathcal{O}_{P}(G)|,

and let

CP=∑G∈𝒟trNP​(G)C_{P}=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}}N_{P}(G)

be its total training-set count. If CP(ℓ)C_{P}^{(\ell)} denotes the corresponding
count in the ℓ\ell-th degree-preserving randomized training set, its
one-sided empirical pp-value is

pP=1+∑ℓ=1L𝟏[CP(ℓ)≥CP]L+1.p_{P}=\frac{1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[C_{P}^{(\ell)}\geq C_{P}]}{L+1}.

Candidates are selected when their Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted pp-value
is at most α\alpha and their observed count exceeds the null mean.

We consider all 29 connected, non-isomorphic unlabeled graphlets with q∈{3,4,5}q\in\{3,4,5\}. Their induced occurrences are enumerated exactly over each seed-specific training split using igraph’s ESU implementation (Wernicke and Rasche, 2006).

Each null replica independently rewires every training graph from its original state using simple-graph double-edge swaps, with 60​|E⁡(G)|60|E(G)| attempts. Degrees are preserved, while self-loops and parallel edges are forbidden. We generate L=500L=500 replicas with seed 31415. A candidate is selected when its Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted enrichment pp-value is at most 0.050.05 and its observed count exceeds the null mean. No vocabulary cap is imposed. We repeat motif discovery independently for each evaluation seed. For seed rr, candidate counts, degree-preserving null graphs, and motif selection are computed using only the corresponding training split Dtr(r)D_{\mathrm{tr}}^{(r)}. The resulting vocabulary 𝒫(r)\mathcal{P}^{(r)} and motif-normalization statistics are frozen and applied to the validation and test graphs of the same split. No validation or test graph is used for motif discovery or normalization.

For each selected motif, the mean and population standard deviation of log⁡(1+hv​m)\log(1+h_{vm}) are computed over all training nodes. Standard deviations below 10−810^{-8} are set to one. The vocabulary and normalization statistics are then frozen. Synthetic effect recovery bypasses discovery and uses a fixed vocabulary containing the three-node path and triangle.

B.3 Training and Model Selection

All models are trained with AdamW for at most 500 epochs, with early-stopping patience 50. We use binary cross-entropy with logits for classification and mean squared error on standardized targets for regression. Graphs are shuffled at each epoch, and gradients are clipped to a global ℓ2\ell_{2} norm of five. For 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA}, graph-balanced centering statistics are refreshed before training and after each epoch.

Checkpoints are selected using the validation objective employed during training. Improvements smaller than 10−610^{-6} are ignored, and the best checkpoint is restored for test evaluation. Final regression performance is reported as MAE in the original target scale.

For each dataset, we randomly sample and evaluate 30 hyperparameter configurations for 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA}, GNAN, GCN, GIN, and Graph Transformer. Configurations are sampled from the search space defined by depth {2,3}\{2,3\}, hidden dimension {16,32,64}\{16,32,64\}, dropout {0,0.05,0.10}\{0,0.05,0.10\}, learning rate η∈{10−3,10−2,10−1}\eta\in\{10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}, weight decay λwd∈{10−5,10−4}\lambda_{\mathrm{wd}}\in\{10^{-5},10^{-4}\}, and batch size {128,256,512}\{128,256,512\}. For 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA}, the search space additionally includes the sparsity coefficient λsp∈{10−3,10−2,10−1}\lambda_{\mathrm{sp}}\in\{10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}

For G-NAMRFF, we apply the same random-search protocol, independently sampling and evaluating 30 configurations for each dataset. Its search space contains M∈{20,40,50,100,200}M\in\{20,40,50,100,200\} random Fourier features, R∈{1,…,7}R\in\{1,\ldots,7\} graph-filter steps, batch size {128,256,512}\{128,256,512\}, and learning rate η∈{10−3,10−2,10−1}\eta\in\{10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}. The remaining parameters are sampled as

θ∼LogUniform⁡(1,4),λwd∼LogUniform⁡(10−5,10−2).\theta\sim\operatorname{LogUniform}(1,4),\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{wd}}\sim\operatorname{LogUniform}(10^{-5},10^{-2}).

For every dataset and model family, all 30 randomly sampled configurations are evaluated under the training protocol described above and ranked by their mean best-validation score. The selected configuration is then retrained from scratch for each of the five evaluation seeds listed in Section B.1, using the train/validation/test split generated by the corresponding seed.

B.4 Explanation Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate node-level plausibility on every test graph. Ground-truth positive nodes are the five vertices of the planted motif in BA-Reg 1 and the union of all planted-motif vertices in BA-Reg 2; all remaining nodes are negative.

For 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA}, nodes are ranked by the magnitude of their native contribution,

sv𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠=|Fv+Sv+Iv|,s_{v}^{\mathsf{AMIGA}}=|F_{v}+S_{v}+I_{v}|,

without allocating the graph intercept. For GNAN Bechler-Speicher et al. (2024) and G-NAMRFF Siddartha Reddy et al. (2025), we use the node-level relevance scores defined by their respective papers, following the original aggregation, baseline, and target-rescaling conventions.

GNNExplainer and Integrated Gradients explain the corresponding trained GIN. GNNExplainer uses PyG 2.7.0 with one object mask per node and no edge mask. The mask is optimized for 200 Adam steps with learning rate 0.010.01, using size and entropy penalties 1.01.0 and 0.10.1. Integrated Gradients uses an all-zero raw-feature baseline and 128 trapezoidal integration intervals. Signed feature attributions are summed per node before taking their absolute value.

For each seed, AUROC is computed separately for every graph in the corresponding test split and then averaged across test graphs with equal weight, yielding one aggregate score per seed. Table 1 reports the mean and sample standard deviation of these aggregate scores across the five training seeds {11,22,33,44,55}\{11,22,33,44,55\}.

B.5 Synthetic Recovery Details

For each of the five seeds {11,22,33,44,55}\{11,22,33,44,55\}, an independent 1,400/300/300 training/validation/test split is generated and the model is trained from scratch, with checkpoint selection based on validation RMSE.

The five runs use training seeds {11,22,33,44,55}\{11,22,33,44,55\}. Observation noise
is generated independently with seed 8193 and standard deviation
σ=1.2312\sigma=1.2312, equal to 10% of the population standard deviation of
the clean training targets.

The experiment uses a fixed 𝖠𝖬𝖨𝖦𝖠\mathsf{AMIGA} configuration with 11–3232–11 scalar
MLPs, ReLU activations, and no dropout. Training uses AdamW with learning
rate 3×10−33\times 10^{-3}, weight decay 10−510^{-5}, sparsity coefficient
10−410^{-4}, batch size 128, and gradient clipping at five. Models train for
at most 600 epochs with patience 100, and the checkpoint with the lowest
validation MSE is restored.

Appendix C Additional Experiments

Table 7 isolates the feature (FF), structural (SS),
and feature–motif interaction (II) channels. The full model obtains
the best mean performance on nine of the ten datasets. The two synthetic
tasks expose the distinct roles of structure and interaction. BA-Reg 1
depends on node features within planted motifs: adding II reduces MAE
from 449.50449.50 to 10.4410.44, while combining all channels further reduces it
to 4.134.13. In contrast, BA-Reg 2 depends directly on the number of planted
motifs: adding SS reduces MAE from 5.14925.1492 to 0.00970.0097, whereas F+IF+I
does not improve over the feature-only model. On the real-world
benchmarks, the relative benefits of SS and II are task-dependent, but
their combination generally provides the strongest performance.
NCI1 is the only exception, where F+IF+I marginally outperforms the
full model.
