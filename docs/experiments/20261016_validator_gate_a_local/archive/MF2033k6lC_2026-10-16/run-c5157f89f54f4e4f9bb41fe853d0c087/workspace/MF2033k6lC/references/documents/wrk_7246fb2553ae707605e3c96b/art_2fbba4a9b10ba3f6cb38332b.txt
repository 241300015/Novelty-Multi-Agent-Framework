When Missingness Follows the Graph: Beyond Feature Propagation for Missing Features

Enrico Chialà1, Francesco Ferrini2, Antonio Longa3, Bruno Lepri2,

Filippo Maria Bianchi3, Manfred Jaeger4, Veronica Lachi3

1University of Trento 2Fondazione Bruno Kessler
3UiT, The Arctic University of Norway 4Aalborg University

Abstract

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) commonly assume that node features are fully observed, despite missing features being pervasive in real-world applications.
By leveraging the dependence between node features and graph structure, existing graph-specific methods handle missingness by propagating observed feature values along graph edges.
However, these methods have mainly been evaluated in overly optimistic settings, where feature entries are removed independently and uniformly across the graph.
This overlooks realistic scenarios in which missingness propagates among connected nodes, as can occur during localized sensing failures in sensor networks. We formalize this complementary notion as graph-structured mask dependence, where the joint distribution of missingness indicators is shaped by the graph, and introduce a controlled mechanism that instantiates this setting.
We study propagation-based methods theoretically and empirically in this setting, showing that their effectiveness changes substantially compared with the uniformly random regime.
Motivated by this limitation, we propose PEMix, designed to remain effective under challenging graph-structured missingness. Rather than propagating observed values, PEMix uses global structural representations to condition a probabilistic model of incomplete node features before message passing. Experiments under controlled graph-structured missingness and naturally occurring missingness show that PEMix remains robust across complex missingness regimes, with particularly strong performance as missingness becomes severe.

1 Introduction

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are commonly designed under the assumption that node features are fully observed (Kipf and Welling, 2017; Hamilton et al., 2017; Velickovic et al., 2018).
In practice, this assumption is often violated.
Sensors may fail to record part of their measurements (Braem et al., 2024; Faizin et al., 2019), while communication, acquisition, or infrastructure failures can also lead to missing observations.

Naive approaches rely on simple statistics to fill missing data, treating the feature matrix as tabular (Ferrini et al., 2026; Taguchi et al., 2021).
Graph-specific methods instead leverage the graph topology to handle missingness more effectively.
Most existing methods estimate missing features by propagating the observed values while minimizing a Dirichlet
energy (Rossi et al., 2022).
Methods such as PCFI and FISF extend this principle with confidence-based and iterative propagation schemes (Um et al., 2023; Um et al., 2025) and achieve strong empirical performance on downstream node-level tasks.

However, performance is mostly evaluated on an oversimplified family of missingness patterns.
The standard protocol removes feature entries independently under uniform Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) (Rubin, 1976).
More recent work has considered feature- and label-dependent mechanisms, including Missing Not At Random (MNAR) settings (Ferrini et al., 2026), but these protocols still focus on how missingness depends on feature values or labels.
They leave unexplored a different aspect of the problem: how missingness is conditioned by topology.
The latter is particularly relevant in applications where failures propagate locally.
In sensor networks or smart grids, physical or communication faults often cause entire connected regions of nodes to drop their measured features simultaneously (Marisca et al., 2024).
Consequently, missingness indicators violate standard spatial independence assumptions, generating correlated missing patterns across adjacent nodes.
Thus, the same global missingness rate can lead to very different levels of difficulty for graph-based methods depending on how missing entries are distributed over the graph.
These patterns challenge propagation-based methods: when missing-feature nodes cluster together, there is too little observed information to propagate. We analyze this limitation theoretically and empirically and we propose PEMix, designed to
remain effective under challenging graph-structured missingness.

Figure 1: Conceptual comparison of missingness mechanisms.
Left (U-MCAR): Feature entries are dropped independently and uniformly across the graph.
Middle (S-MCAR): Entire feature vectors are missing for randomly sampled nodes, independent of connectivity.
Right (Recursive Transmission, RT): Missingness originates at a shared seed node and expands recursively along graph edges, forming spatially correlated missing regions that challenge propagation-based recovery.

Our contributions are as follows.

1.

We distinguish the classical dependence of the missingness mask on the data from the dependence structure within the mask itself, formalize
graph-structured mask dependence, and introduce Recursive Transmission (RT), a controlled graph-structured mechanism.

2.

We establish a boundary bottleneck for standard feature propagation and show empirically that graph-localized missingness challenges more sophisticated propagation methods.

3.

We propose PEMix to handle clustered missingness where propagation-based approaches fail. It conditions a probabilistic feature model on global structural representations prior to message passing, incorporating graph topology directly without relying on value propagation.

4.

Across controlled graph-structured missingness and real-world datasets, we show that PEMix improves robustness over propagation baselines, achieving particularly strong gains as missingness severity increases.

Some methods handle missing node features without using
topological information during feature recovery. They operate only on
the incomplete feature matrix, typically by replacing missing entries in 𝐗mis\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{mis}}
with statistics computed from the observed values of the same feature in 𝐗obs\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{obs}},
such as the mean or median.
For example, GCNmf models the feature distribution with
a Gaussian mixture and uses the resulting conditional moments to handle
missing coordinates (Taguchi et al., 2021). After filling missing
entries with placeholders, GNN-MIM instead concatenates the missingness
mask to the node features (Ferrini et al., 2026). In these
approaches, the graph is still used by the downstream GNN, but
missing features are not recovered using any topological information.

On the other hand, topology-aware approaches such as Feature Propagation (FP)
(Rossi et al., 2022), estimates missing feature

(3)

PCFI extends this principle by assigning confidence scores based on the
distance from observed features and combining channel-wise diffusion
with inter-channel information (Um et al., 2023). FISF
alternates propagation with repeated injection of the observed features
(Um et al., 2025). Although these methods differ in their
implementation, they all recover missing feature by propagating observed feature values
through graph connections.

A crucial point often neglected in the literature is that the mechanisms used to describe the missingness are often orthogonal to how missingness is recovered.
The latter is described by the missingness mechanism pλp_{\lambda} in equation 1. Following Rubin
(Rubin, 1976), missingness mechanisms can be characterized
through conditional dependencies between the mask and the data. Since
𝐀\mathbf{A} is fully observed and fixed in our setting, we express these
conditions through 𝐀\mathbf{A}.

For node classification, a mechanism is feature-MAR when

p⁡(𝐌∣𝐗mis,𝐗obs,𝐀)=p⁡(𝐌∣𝐗obs,𝐀),p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{mis}},\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{A})=p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{A}),

(4)

i.e., the missingness only depends on the observed features. On the other hand, is
label-MAR when

p⁡(𝐌∣𝐗,𝐘,𝐀)=p⁡(𝐌∣𝐗,𝐀),p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{A})=p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A}),

(5)

and Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) when

p⁡(𝐌∣𝐗,𝐘,𝐀)=p⁡(𝐌∣𝐀).p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{A})=p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{A}).

(6)

As in Ferrini et al. (2026) we treat the observed graph structure as fixed throughout learning and inference. Hence, MCAR refers to independence of the missingness mask from the random data variables XX and YY, conditional on the realized graph AA.
Since 𝐀\mathbf{A} is fixed, equation 6 is equivalent to
the usual statement that the mask is independent of the data
(𝐗,𝐘)(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}). Missingness is MNAR when the mask depends on hidden feature values or labels. Under feature-MAR and label-MAR, the missingness mechanism is ignorable
for prediction and equation 2 reduces to

p⁡(𝐘∣𝐗obs,𝐌,𝐀)=∫pγ​(𝐘∣𝐗,𝐀)​pθ​(𝐗mis∣𝐗obs,𝐀)​d​𝐗mis.p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{M},\mathbf{A})=\int p_{\gamma}(\mathbf{Y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A})\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{A})\,d\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{mis}}.

(7)

To assess robustness to missing features, existing methods are commonly evaluated by injecting
missingness into complete graph datasets. The standard
protocol assumes uniform MCAR (U-MCAR), where each feature entry is
removed independently with a fixed probability μ\mu,
Mi​j​∼i.i.d.​Bernoulli​(μ)M_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\mu).
Another commonly used MCAR protocol removes the complete feature vectors of uniformly selected nodes; following
Ferrini et al. (2026), we refer to this setting as Structural
MCAR (S-MCAR).

The MCAR/MAR/MNAR taxonomy characterizes how 𝐌\mathbf{M} depends on the data, but not how the entries of 𝐌\mathbf{M} depend on one another. This distinction is crucial for graph-based recovery: two mechanisms can be equally MCAR and share the same global missingness rate, yet arrange missing entries according to the graph topology. This spatial arrangement directly affects propagation-based methods, as the configuration of observed entries determines the information available for diffusion. In the next section, we formalize this structural missingness when the graph shapes the mask.

3 Graph-Structured Missingness

To study how graph topology conditions missingness patterns, we first formalize the internal joint organization of the mask MM. Let p⁡(𝐌∣𝐀)p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{A}) denote the joint distribution of the missingness mask conditional on the observed adjacency matrix 𝐀\mathbf{A}.
We say that the mask is internally dependent when its distribution does not factorize over individual entries:

p⁡(𝐌∣𝐀)≠∏i=1n∏j=1dp⁡(𝐌i​j∣𝐀).p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{A})\neq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{d}p(\mathbf{M}_{ij}\mid\mathbf{A}).

(8)

Equation 6 constrains the dependence between 𝐌\mathbf{M} and the
data (𝐗,𝐘)(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}), whereas
Equation 8 concerns dependence among entries
of 𝐌\mathbf{M}: for example, consider an MCAR mechanism that masks exactly
mm out of the N=n​dN=nd feature entries uniformly without replacement. For any two distinct entries a=i​ja=ij and b=z​kb=zk,
Pr⁡(𝐌a=1∣𝐀)=mN\Pr(\mathbf{M}_{a}=1\mid\mathbf{A})=\frac{m}{N}, while
Pr⁡(𝐌b=1∣𝐌a=1,𝐀)=m−1N−1≠mN\Pr(\mathbf{M}_{b}=1\mid\mathbf{M}_{a}=1,\mathbf{A})=\frac{m-1}{N-1}\neq\frac{m}{N}.
Thus, fixing the global missingness budget already induces dependence among mask entries and is a broad property. In this work, we are interested in a narrower
case in which the graph itself shapes this dependence structure, which is a realistic and challenging scenario.

3.1 Graph-Structured Dependence within the Missingness Mask

We characterize graph-structured dependence at the level of the joint mask distribution.

Definition 1 (Graph-structured mask dependence).

Let p⁡(𝐌∣𝐀)p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{A}) denote the conditional mask distribution. The missing mechanism exhibits graph-structured mask
dependence if

1.

the internal dependency on the mask is conditional on 𝐀\mathbf{A} and

2.

the joint mask distribution depends on graph structure, i.e.,
there exist possible adjacency matrices 𝐀\mathbf{A} and 𝐀′\mathbf{A}^{\prime} on the same node set, each with positive probability, such that
p⁡(𝐌∣𝐀)≠p⁡(𝐌∣𝐀′)p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{A})\neq p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{A}^{\prime}),
with all non-topological parameters of the mechanism held fixed.

This definition is broad enough to encompass the missingness mechanisms from the literature: U-MCAR without fixed budget is MCAR
with internally independent mask; fixed-budget U-MCAR induces internally dependent masks without graph-structured dependence.
Finally, S-MCAR also induces strong within-node dependence by masking complete feature vectors, but the
masked nodes are sampled independently of 𝐀\mathbf{A}. These standard protocols do not model graph-structured dependencies among mask entries. In the following, we introduce a mechanism designed to account for such dependencies.

3.2 Recursive-Transmission MCAR

Graph-structured mask dependence can be instantiated in many ways.
We propose a mechanism for graph-structured missingness called Recursive Transmission (RT) that: i) is directly comparable with
standard MCAR protocols at the same missingness rate; ii) generates a missingness mask that can
challenge existing propagation-based methods; iii) captures a plausible class of localized failure patterns. For a target missingness rate μ\mu, let m=⌊μ​n​d⌋m=\lfloor\mu nd\rfloor
be the total number of entries to mask. We distribute this budget as
evenly as possible across each feature channel jj, obtaining budgets mjm_{j}
such that ∑j=1dmj=m\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}=m.
RT starts from a source node s∈Vs\in V shared across feature channels
and expands each feature-specific missing region only through graph
edges. For feature jj with mj>0m_{j}>0, initialize Cj(1)={s}C_{j}^{(1)}=\{s\}. At iteration tt, define
∂Cj(t)={v∉Cj(t):∃u∈Cj(t)​ with ​(u,v)∈E},\partial C_{j}^{(t)}=\left\{v\notin C_{j}^{(t)}:\exists u\in C_{j}^{(t)}\text{ with }(u,v)\in E\right\},
and, for each candidate v∈∂Cj(t)v\in\partial C_{j}^{(t)},
aj(t)​(v)=|𝒩⁡(v)∩Cj(t)|a_{j}^{(t)}(v)=\left|\mathcal{N}(v)\cap C_{j}^{(t)}\right|.

channels. We then set Cj(t+1)=Cj(t)∪{vt+1(j)}C_{j}^{(t+1)}=C_{j}^{(t)}\cup\{v_{t+1}^{(j)}\},
and continue until |Cj|=mj|C_{j}|=m_{j}. Finally,
𝐌i​j=𝕀{i∈Cj}\mathbf{M}_{ij}=\mathbb{I}\{i\in C_{j}\}.
The structural differences between missingness protocols are illustrated in Figure 1.
While U-MCAR removes feature entries uniformly at random and S-MCAR drops complete feature vectors for independently selected nodes, RT models localized failures that originate from a shared source node and expand along graph edges, generating compact, spatially correlated missing regions.
The construction is motivated by the three goals above. First, RT
represents a localized failure with a common origin, as may occur in
sensor, communication, power-grid, or acquisition systems, while
feature-specific growth allows different measurements to be affected along different trajectories.

Among the possible instantiations of the mechanism, we focus on the challenging case in which the seeds of the missingness of each feature coincide with the same source node.
In general, in RT one could assign a distinct seed ss to each feature.
However, on large graphs, especially at moderate missingness rates and with relatively few features, this would place feature-specific missing regions in different parts of the graph.
Many affected nodes would then lose only one or a few measurements, while retaining most of their feature vector.
While such masks still contain graph-structured dependence, they describe a missingness that is easier to recover for methods that can exploit nearby observations or even just information from the other feature channels.
On the other hand, by using the same seed for all channels, RT creates partially overlapping missing regions and therefore areas in which several measurements are simultaneously unavailable.
Independent tie-breaking still allows the regions to diverge, avoiding to always end up in the more restrictive case where the same complete
set of features is missing at every affected node.

Finally, using a single global rate μ\mu makes the severity of RT
directly comparable with fixed-budget U-MCAR and S-MCAR. The mechanisms can therefore be matched in the total number of missing entries while differing in how those entries are arranged over the graph.
RT is not intended as a generative model of all real-world missingness patterns. Rather, it is a controlled stress test that isolates the effect of graph-localized mask dependence while matching standard protocols in the total missingness budget. In our experimental setting, we therefore complement RT with experiments on naturally occurring missingness, for which no graph-structured mechanism is imposed.

Proposition 1 (RT is graph-structured MCAR).

If source selection and tie-breaking are independent of 𝐗\mathbf{X} and 𝐘\mathbf{Y} conditional on 𝐀\mathbf{A},
then RT is MCAR and satisfies
Definition 1.

A formal proof is provided in
Appendix A. RT is thus a realistic graph-structured MCAR mechanism whose particular geometry
is chosen to probe a failure mode of propagation-based methods. We
make this connection precise next.

3.3 Why Graph-Structured Missingness Is Difficult for Propagation

We now show why RT can be particularly challenging for methods that
recover missing features through graph propagation. Consider one feature channel and omit the feature index for readability.
Let OO and MM denote the observed and missing nodes for that feature,

4 PEMix: Structure-Conditioned Probabilistic Imputation

The analysis in Section 3.3 shows that
propagation-based methods can become less effective when missingness propagates
through graph connections. The graph, however, remains fully observed and can
still provide information about node attributes. PEMix exploits this
information without using topology to propagate observed feature values into
missing regions.

Let 𝐏=g⁡(𝐀)∈ℝn×q\mathbf{P}=g(\mathbf{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q} be a fixed structural
representation of the graph, with 𝐩i\mathbf{p}_{i} denoting the representation of
node ii. In our experiments, gg returns standardized non-trivial Laplacian
eigenvectors. PEMix uses 𝐏\mathbf{P} to condition a probabilistic model of the
incomplete node features,
pθ​(𝐗mis∣𝐗obs,𝐀)≈qη​(𝐗mis∣𝐗obs,𝐏)p_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{A})\approx q_{\eta}(\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{P}).
The positional encodings are computed once from the fully observed graph and
are not learned by the mixture.

Structure-Conditioned Mixture

For each node ii, let 𝐱i=𝐗i,:∈ℝd\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{X}_{i,:}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} denote its
feature vector and let ci∈{1,…,K}c_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,K\} denote a latent mixture
component, with q⁡(ci=k)=πkq(c_{i}=k)=\pi_{k}. Conditioned on cic_{i}, PEMix models node
features and structural position independently with diagonal Gaussian
distributions,
qη​(𝐱i∣ci=k)=𝒩⁡(𝐱i∣𝝁kX,diag⁡((𝝈kX)2))q_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid c_{i}=k)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\bm{\mu}_{k}^{X},\operatorname{diag}((\bm{\sigma}_{k}^{X})^{2}))
and
qη​(𝐩i∣ci=k)=𝒩⁡(𝐩i∣𝝁kP,diag⁡((𝝈kP)2))q_{\eta}(\mathbf{p}_{i}\mid c_{i}=k)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{p}_{i}\mid\bm{\mu}_{k}^{P},\operatorname{diag}((\bm{\sigma}_{k}^{P})^{2})).

Let

denote the observed coordinates of node ii, and let

(12)

with normalization over kk. Equivalently, the structural term induces a

Thus, graph structure affects the distribution used to impute missing

End-to-End Prediction

PEMix retains the conditional uncertainty in Equation
13 instead of committing to a single point estimate.
Following Taguchi et al. (2021), the corresponding component-wise
moments are marginalized analytically through the first message-passing layer,
after which the remaining GNN layers are unchanged. The positional encodings
affect only the mixture responsibilities in
equation 12; they are not concatenated to the node
representation passed to the downstream GNN.
Following (Taguchi et al., 2021), who showed that jointly optimizing the mixture and downstream GNN can improve missing-feature reconstruction, we initialize the mixture parameters with EM and subsequently optimize them jointly with the GNN using the downstream task loss. We validate this design choice in Section F.3, where joint optimization consistently improves downstream performance compared with keeping the EM-initialized mixture fixed.
Implementation details, including initialization,
covariance parameterization, and positional-encoding dimension, are provided
in Appendix E. Structural conditioning does not change the
asymptotic cost of the probabilistic first layer: PEMix has the same per-epoch
complexity as GCNmf, up to a dominated O⁡(n​K​q)O(nKq) term and a one-off partial
eigendecomposition per graph (Section E.1).

5 Experimental Evaluation

We address three research questions:

•

RQ1: Does PEMix improve robustness under challenging graph-structured missingness while remaining competitive under standard random missingness?

•

RQ2: How does PEMix perform under naturally occurring missingness?

•

RQ3: Are the gains of PEMix due simply to access to positional encodings, or specifically to using them to condition the missing-feature distribution?

Datasets and protocol.

To answer RQ1, we consider four datasets with artificially injected missingness, namely TADPOLE (Zhu et al., 2019), GraphMSO, PEMS-BAY and Engrad (Marisca et al., 2024). Since these datasets are complete, the missingness
mechanism and global missingness rate μ\mu can be controlled directly. In particular we injected RT and U-MCAR with μ∈{0,0.1,…,0.9,0.99}\mu\in\{0,0.1,\ldots,0.9,0.99\}. For RQ2, we instead consider datasets where missingness is part of the original
data. AQI, PV-US, and METR-LA are the air-quality, photovoltaic, and traffic datasets used by Marisca et al. (2024). We additionally evaluate
PEMix in a different setting using RelBench (Robinson et al., 2024), a benchmark of
relational databases with naturally incomplete features. For forecasting datasets, we convert each prediction time into an independent static graph sample using only past observations; the temporal windows, graph construction, and leakage-prevention protocol are detailed in Appendix B. The dataset overview is provided in Appendix B, Table 5. On the standard graph datasets, we compare against simple baseline with zero imputation (GNN-Zero), median imputation (GNN-Median) and mean imputation (GNN-MI), probabilistic and mask-aware approaches
(GCNmf and GNN-MIM), and propagation methods (FP, PCFI, and FISF).
RelBench requires a relational architecture and has substantially fewer
missing-feature baselines. We therefore compare against the standard Relational
Deep Learning model (RDL) (Robinson et al., 2024) and its missing-indicator extension RDL-MIM (Ferrini et al., 2026), and
adapt PEMix to the same backbone, denoted RDL-PEMix. The datasets and evaluation
regimes are summarized in Table 5.

RQ1: PEMix gains most where graph-structured missingness is challenging, without sacrificing competitiveness under U-MCAR.
 For each controlled dataset, at each missingness rate, we evaluate all methods over five
random seeds. The complete curves, including means and standard deviations at
each rate, are reported in Appendix D. For compactness, in
Table 1 we summarize each curve by its trapezoidal area under
the curve (AUC) over the evaluated missingness rates. Higher AUC is better for
macro-F1, while lower AUC is better for MAE. Under RT, the setting PEMix is explicitly designed to address, PEMix obtains the best AUC on three of the four datasets: TADPOLE, GraphMSO, and PEMS-BAY, while ranking third on Engrad. The gains are particularly pronounced on TADPOLE and PEMS-BAY, where PEMix improves substantially over the strongest competing baselines. This supports the intended role of structural conditioning when missing observations form graph-localized regions and local propagation becomes less informative. Under U-MCAR, in contrast, we would not necessarily expect PEMix to dominate, since missing entries are scattered independently and propagation-based or simpler recovery mechanisms are not exposed to the same structural bottleneck. Nevertheless, PEMix remains competitive: it ranks first on TADPOLE and GraphMSO, second on PEMS-BAY, and third on EngRAD. Thus, the benefit of PEMix is concentrated in the challenging graph-structured regime for which it was designed, while its use does not generally entail a loss of competitiveness when missingness is unstructured.

To isolate the role of positional encodings, we compare two alternatives.

6 Conclusion

We studied missing node features when missingness itself is structured by the graph. We formalized graph-structured mask dependence and introduced RT, a controlled mechanism that exposes a boundary bottleneck of propagation-based method. Motivated by this limitation, we proposed PEMix, which conditions a probabilistic model of incomplete node features on global structural representations. Across controlled and naturally occurring missingness, PEMix is particularly effective when missing observations are graph-localized, while remaining competitive under standard missingness. Ablations further show that the gains arise from using structural information to condition the missing-feature distribution, rather than from access to positional encodings alone. These results highlight graph structure as a useful source of information not only for prediction, but also for modeling incomplete node features. Limitations: PEMix assumes diagonal mixture covariances and a fully observed graph, and our evaluation is limited to node classification and forecasting. Broader analyses of structural encodings, temporal modeling, and other downstream tasks are left for future work.

Appendix A Proofs

A.1 RT is graph-structured MCAR

Proposition 1 (RT is graph-structured MCAR), restated.
If source selection and tie-breaking are independent of 𝐗\mathbf{X} and 𝐘\mathbf{Y} conditional on 𝐀\mathbf{A},
then RT is MCAR in the sense of equation 6. Under the non-degeneracy and graph-support conditions stated in Appendix A, it also satisfies
Definition 1.

Proof.

We work conditional on the realized adjacency matrix 𝐀\mathbf{A} and fixed feature budgets, as in the main text. For the graph-structured conclusion, we make the necessary non-degeneracy conditions explicit: the graph is connected and undirected, the source distribution has full support on VV, and some channel has 2≤mj≤n−12\leq m_{j}\leq n-1. For the topology comparison below, the complete graph and the star on VV are assumed to have positive probability under the distribution of 𝐀\mathbf{A}. In comparing graphs, the source law and all budgets are held fixed. These conditions are needed for that conclusion: at zero or full missingness the mask is deterministic, and if all budgets are at most one, no growth step need consult an edge. Thus independence from the data alone proves MCAR, while the graph-structured assertion is understood in this non-degenerate regime.

MCAR. Let UU collect the source and all tie-breaking variables. The recursion is a deterministic map 𝐌=F⁡(𝐀,U)\mathbf{M}=F(\mathbf{A},U): each update uses only adjacency, the current region, and UU. Since UU is independent of (𝐗,𝐘)(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}) conditional on 𝐀\mathbf{A}, for every mask mm,

Pr⁡(𝐌=m∣𝐗,𝐘,𝐀)=Pr⁡(F⁡(𝐀,U)=m∣𝐀)=Pr⁡(𝐌=m∣𝐀).\Pr(\mathbf{M}=m\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{A})=\Pr(F(\mathbf{A},U)=m\mid\mathbf{A})=\Pr(\mathbf{M}=m\mid\mathbf{A}).

This is Equation 6; dependence within the mask does not affect this conditional independence from the data.

Internal dependence. Fix a channel with 2≤mj≤n−12\leq m_{j}\leq n-1. Every node has positive probability of being masked, since it can be the source. The fixed budget implies ∑iMi​j=mj<n\sum_{i}M_{ij}=m_{j}<n almost surely, so at least one indicator also has positive probability of being zero. If the channel indicators were mutually independent, then

0=Var⁡(∑iMi​j∣𝐀)=∑iVar⁡(Mi​j∣𝐀)>0,0=\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\sum_{i}M_{ij}\mid\mathbf{A}\right)=\sum_{i}\operatorname{Var}(M_{ij}\mid\mathbf{A})>0,

which is impossible. Independence of every entry of the full mask would imply independence within this channel. The conditional distribution p⁡(𝐌∣𝐀)p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{A}) therefore does not factorize.

Dependence on topology. Compare the complete graph with adjacency matrix 𝐀\mathbf{A} on the labeled set VV with a star centered at aa with adjacency matrix 𝐀′\mathbf{A}^{\prime}, holding the full-support source law, budgets, and uniform tie-breaking rule fixed. Choose S⊆V∖{a}S\subseteq V\setminus\{a\} with |S|=mj|S|=m_{j}. In the complete graph all boundary candidates have the same score. Starting in SS and then selecting its remaining nodes has positive probability, so Pr⁡(Cj=S∣𝐀)>0\Pr(C_{j}=S\mid\mathbf{A})>0. On the star every RT region is connected, because it starts with one node and grows by adding boundary nodes. A set of at least two leaves excluding the center is disconnected, so Pr⁡(Cj=S∣𝐀′)=0\Pr(C_{j}=S\mid\mathbf{A}^{\prime})=0. The conditional channel marginals, and hence the joint mask distributions p⁡(𝐌∣𝐀)p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{A}) and p⁡(𝐌∣𝐀′)p(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbf{A}^{\prime}), differ. Together with internal dependence, this proves Definition 1 under the stated non-degeneracy conditions.
∎

A.2 Boundary bottleneck for feature propagation

Proposition 2 (Boundary bottleneck for feature propagation), restated.
Let ρ⁡(𝐀^M​M)\rho(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{MM}) denote the spectral radius of
𝐀^M​M\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{MM} and assume

Proof.

Conditional on the realized graph, the normalized adjacency is a fixed local propagation matrix: an off-diagonal entry can be nonzero only on a graph edge. Observations xOx_{O} remain clamped. Set T=𝐀^M​MT=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{MM} and C=𝐀^M​OC=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{MO}. For every integer t≥1t\geq 1, induction on the update gives

xM(t)=Tt​xM(0)+∑r=0t−1Tr​C​xO.x_{M}^{(t)}=T^{t}x_{M}^{(0)}+\sum_{r=0}^{t-1}T^{r}Cx_{O}.

Consequently Tt→0T^{t}\to 0. Also I−TI-T is invertible, and the finite geometric-series identity gives

Appendix B Datasets and Evaluation Protocol

Static formulation of forecasting datasets.

For AQI, PV-US, METR-LA, and GraphMSO, we convert each prediction time into an independent static graph sample. The graph topology is fixed over time and constructed from sensor locations, official distance files, or the GraphMSO generator, depending on the dataset; it never uses future observations. Node features contain only past sensor measurements, flattened over the input window, together with unmasked calendar covariates when available. Targets are the subsequent measurements. The input/output windows are 24/6 for AQI, 72/6 for PV-US, 24/12 for METR-LA, and 72/36 for GraphMSO. No temporal model is used: all methods operate on the resulting static graph samples with the same inputs.
To prevent leakage, train/validation/test splits are chronological and windows are constructed so that each input–target pair lies entirely within one split. Standardization and imputation statistics are estimated from observed training values only. Artificial masks are generated on the raw series before windowing, so repeated occurrences of the same measurement share the same missingness state. Naturally missing values are preserved, and missing targets are excluded from both loss and evaluation. PEMix positional encodings depend only on the fixed graph and are computed once per dataset; propagation-based imputers operate only on sensor measurements, not on calendar covariates.

B.1 Spatial organization of missingness in real-world datasets

We test whether naturally occurring missing values fall on neighboring nodes more often than they
would if missingness were spatially independent.

Co-missingness ratio.

Let Mt​i​j=1M_{tij}=1 if feature jj of node ii is missing at timestamp tt. For each feature jj and
timestamp tt, let

pt​j=1n∑i=1nMt​i​j,ht​jmiss=1|E|∑(u,v)∈E𝕀[Mt​u​j=1∧Mt​v​j=1]p_{tj}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{tij},\qquad h^{\mathrm{miss}}_{tj}=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\mathbb{I}\!\left[M_{tuj}=1\land M_{tvj}=1\right]

denote the missingness rate and the fraction of edges whose two endpoints are both missing.
If missingness were independent across nodes with the same rate pt​jp_{tj}, the expected fraction
of missing–missing edges would be pt​j2p_{tj}^{2}. Over a set of timestamps 𝒯\mathcal{T}, we
therefore define

H​Cmiss​(j)=∑t∈𝒯ht​jmiss∑t∈𝒯pt​j2,HC_{\mathrm{miss}}(j)=\frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}h^{\mathrm{miss}}_{tj}}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}p_{tj}^{2}},

a ratio of sums rather than an average of per-timestamp ratios. H​Cmiss=1HC_{\mathrm{miss}}=1
corresponds to spatial independence, and values above 11 indicate excess co-missingness between
neighboring nodes. Each dataset has a single measured channel (PM2.5 for AQI, power for PV-US,
and speed for METR-LA), so the dataset-level value is that of its channel.

Evaluation subsets.

We compute the ratio over all timestamps and over partial timestamps, those with
0<pt​j<10<p_{tj}<1. Completely observed and completely missing snapshots carry no spatial contrast.
This distinction matters on METR-LA, where many missing values come from graph-wide outages.

Permutation control.

To check that excess co-missingness reflects graph position rather than marginal missingness
rates, we randomly permute the complete missingness trajectories of nodes across graph positions.
This preserves temporal missingness patterns and the number of missing nodes at each timestamp,
while breaking their association with the topology. We use 999 permutations over all timestamps
and report the central 95%95\% range of the resulting null distribution.

Results.

Appendix E Implementation and Reproducibility Details

This appendix records optimization, covariance assumptions, hyperparameter ranges, and computational complexity. These details are separated from the conceptual method in Section 4 so the main text follows the mechanism-to-model argument.

Fully-missing nodes.

When 𝒪i=∅\mathcal{O}_{i}=\emptyset, the feature contribution to the responsibility
vanishes, while the positional term remains active. Consequently, the
imputation can remain node-specific when the PE-conditioned responsibilities distinguish nodes.

Learning the mixture.

The mixture parameters (πk,μk,σk2)(\pi_{k},\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}) are not fixed by a
separate EM fit; they are trainable and optimised end-to-end with the GCN
weights W(1),W(2)W^{(1)},W^{(2)} by minimising the cross-entropy of the
node-classification loss. We initialise them with a few EM iterations on the
mean-imputed extended matrix Z=[X~∣PE]Z=[\widetilde{X}\mid\mathrm{PE}], which gives a sensible
starting partition, and then let backpropagation refine them jointly with the
classifier. The mixing weights are kept on the simplex by an unconstrained
softmax re-parameterisation, and the variances are kept positive by optimising
log⁡σk2\log\sigma_{k}^{2}.

Diagonal covariances.

We use diagonal covariances diag⁡(σk2)\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{k}^{2}) throughout. This is
what makes the marginalisation of the missing coordinates closed-form and the
expected ReLU in the probabilistic first layer analytic; full covariances would couple observed and missing coordinates and require more expensive conditional-moment computations, although Gaussian conditioning and scalar expected ReLUs remain analytic. The diagonal assumption is the
main modelling limitation of PEMix.

Hyperparameters.

For all competing methods, we follow the hyperparameter search spaces and model-selection
procedures of Ferrini et al. (2026) and of the original methods, using
the official implementations when available. Model selection is performed exclusively on the
validation set, without access to test performance.

For the controlled-missingness experiments, hyperparameter selection is performed independently
for RT and U-MCAR. Within each mechanism, candidate configurations are evaluated over the full
missingness curve and selected according to validation AUC. The selected network and optimization hyperparameters are reported for
