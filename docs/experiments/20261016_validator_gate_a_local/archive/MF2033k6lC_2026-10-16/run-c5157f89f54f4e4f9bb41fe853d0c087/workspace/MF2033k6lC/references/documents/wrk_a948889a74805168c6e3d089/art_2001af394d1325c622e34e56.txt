Abstract

Neural networks operate naturally on finite-dimensional vectors, yet many problems require reasoning about higher-order objects such as distributions, functions, and graphs. Such objects capture relationships within a ground set and rarely admit an explicit compact representation. Moreover, we typically want a compact representation not of a single higher-order object but of an entire family of such objects, including members unseen during training. While a few approaches exist for particular object types, none is designed to work across object types. We propose a unified framework that represents any higher-order object as a fixed-size embedding vector learned over a family of such objects. An encoder maps an object, seen only through a finite set of samples, to an embedding, and a domain-specific decoder supplies the training signal by modeling the object conditioned on that embedding. Since the encoder works only from samples, one architecture serves different object types. Because the decoder is a model already suited to single instances of the object type, the framework inherits that model’s native operations and inductive biases. We instantiate the framework on distributions, functions, and graphs. In each case we show that a single trained model represents an entire family and generalizes to members unseen during training. Furthermore, we demonstrate the downstream utility of these embeddings: interpolating distributions in embedding space, solving differential equations in embedding space, and predicting unseen edges from a graph’s embedding.

1 Introduction

Neural networks have seen a widespread adoption in practically every field of science and engineering where inputs and outputs can be represented as finite-dimensional vectors. In such cases, existing approaches excel at approximating complex relationships between inputs and outputs coming from complex distributions (LeCun et al., 2015; Goodfellow et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2023). However, there is no natural way of capturing distributions, functions, or generally any higher-order objects, as inputs or producing them as outputs. For instance, it is not obvious how to train a neural network to predict the probability density function of an input distribution as there is no general compact representation to provide to the network.

Once we allow higher-order objects as inputs and outputs, it is natural to restrict ourselves to the relevant families of such objects. Examples include families of distributions that share a common parameterization, such as the family of Gaussian distributions parameterized by their mean and covariance, or solutions to differential equations under varying parameters and initial conditions. When a family admits a closed-form parameterization, one can simply feed the parameters into the neural network, but in many cases such a parameterization is not known a priori. Manipulating these higher-order objects, and families thereof, efficiently, with generalization to unseen members, has no general and established solution and is typically handled in ad-hoc and domain-specific manners (Edwards and Storkey, 2016; Kovachki et al., 2023; Narayanan et al., 2017, e.g.,).

These approaches each target a single object type and carry inductive biases that do not transfer to the others. For distributions, kernel mean embeddings (Muandet et al., 2017) depend on a hand-crafted kernel, and the Neural Statistician (Edwards and Storkey, 2016) ties its embeddings to a variational autoencoder. For functions, Neural Processes (Garnelo et al., 2018) learn distributions over functions, and neural operators such as DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021) and FNO (Li et al., 2020) learn maps between function spaces, but impose rigid input assumptions and expose no representation of an individual function. For graphs, most methods embed nodes rather than whole graphs (Hamilton et al., 2017) or learn to generate graphs (Simonovsky and Komodakis, 2018; You et al., 2018; Vignac et al., 2022) without exposing an embedding of any single one. More recently, distribution embeddings have been proposed independently in two distinct domains: as part of a filtering algorithm for state-space models (Solinas et al., 2026b) and as a way to reason across scales in computational biology (Fishman et al., 2025).

We propose a unified framework for representing and learning from families of higher-order objects, building on the distribution embeddings introduced in the initial version of Solinas et al. (2026b). It couples an encoder, which maps an object given only as a finite set of samples to a fixed-size vector, with a domain-specific decoder that provides the training signal. Because the encoder sees only samples, a single architecture handles very different object types; because the decoder is the model one would normally fit to a single object, the framework lifts its native operations, such as sampling, density evaluation, and spectral reconstruction, from one object to a whole family, while producing a compact representation of each member that is reusable in downstream tasks.

We instantiate the framework on three object types, changing primarily the decoder. For distributions, a generative decoder reconstructs densities from few samples, and the embedding space supports arithmetic and interpolation between distributions. For functions, a pointwise decoder reconstructs functions faithfully and scales to high-dimensional state; because whole functions become vectors, an ordinary network acts as a neural operator, which we use to differentiate functions and solve ODEs in embedding space. For graphs, a spectral decoder recovers a Laplacian representation that reliably separates edges from non-edges, from sampled edges alone. In every case, a single trained model generalizes to unseen family members and to sample sets of arbitrary size.

2 Embedding & Learning From Families of Higher-Order Objects

For any given higher-order object, there is usually a well-established way of approximating it — for instance, generative models for distributions (Kingma and Welling, 2013; Papamakarios et al., 2021; Lipman et al., 2022). Such approaches, however, treat every object in isolation: whenever the target changes, the model must be retrained from scratch, and it never yields an explicit compact representation of the object. Our framework instead replaces per-object fitting with a single model that represents a whole family of objects and generalizes to members unseen during training.

Our goal is two-fold: a) to learn representations of higher-order objects, and b) to enable learning across families of such objects. We view a higher-order object 𝒪\mathcal{O} as anything that has no canonical finite-vector representation, such as a distribution, a function, or a graph. A family ℱ\mathcal{F} is a collection of related objects; for example, the solutions of a given differential equation across its initial conditions and parameters form a family.

For a family of higher-order objects to be learnable within our framework, we require
the following three components:

1.

Object sampler. A procedure 𝒮\mathcal{S} that, given an object
𝒪∈ℱ\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{F}, produces a set of (weighted) samples 𝒟\mathcal{D}. This set, if sufficiently large, represents
𝒪\mathcal{O} and is our only access to it; we never require a closed-form description
of the object.

2.

Parameterizable fitting model. A model gψg_{\psi} that can learn
to represent an object 𝒪\mathcal{O} from a set of samples 𝒟\mathcal{D}.

3.

Training loss. A loss function ℒ\mathcal{L} that provides the only
training signal for the framework. It is usually tied to the fitting model gψg_{\psi}.

Figure 1: General training procedure. Each sample set 𝒟\mathcal{D} is split
into two halves, 𝒟embed\mathcal{D}_{\text{embed}} and 𝒟train\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}. The
embedder ℰϕ\mathcal{E}_{\phi} maps 𝒟embed\mathcal{D}_{\text{embed}} to an embedding vector
θ\theta that conditions the object-specific decoder gψg_{\psi}. The held-out
half 𝒟train\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} is then used to score gψg_{\psi} and compute
the training loss ℒ\mathcal{L}. Finally, ℒ\mathcal{L} is differentiated with respect
to the trainable parameters of both networks and backpropagated.

To make the three components concrete, consider a family of two-dimensional, donut-shaped distributions. Each object 𝒪\mathcal{O} is a single donut, fully described by three parameters: a mean, a radius, and a width, although this parameterization is not known a priori. The family ℱ\mathcal{F} is itself a distribution over such donuts. A sample set 𝒟\mathcal{D} for a given donut is a collection of i.i.d. points {xi}i=1n\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}, xi∈ℝ2x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}, drawn from it; Figure 2 shows several donuts, each represented by its own 𝒟\mathcal{D}. The fitting model gψg_{\psi} can be any generative model, such as a Normalizing Flow, and the training loss is the negative log-likelihood of the samples under that model.

Given 𝒮\mathcal{S}, gψg_{\psi}, and ℒ\mathcal{L}, we train a single model that a) learns
to represent the objects in a family as compact embedding vectors θ∈ℝm\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}, from only a partial view of each object through samples, and b) learns to
fit an object given its embedding and an object-specific input. To this end, our
model comprises two jointly-trained neural networks: an embedder
ℰϕ\mathcal{E}_{\phi}, sometimes called an encoder, and the supplied fitting model gψg_{\psi}, which we will refer to as
the decoder.

The role of the embedder is to compress an object’s samples into a fixed-size
vector that retains the information needed to fit the object. Using sets of
samples as the only input has two benefits. First, it lets a single architecture
serve very different object types, as the following sections show. Second,
it lets us handle objects seen only through samples, at arbitrary sample-set
sizes, including objects never encountered during training. The fixed-size
embedding is convenient in turn: it can serve as a conditioning signal, or
as an input to and output of other networks. Our framework conditions the decoder
gψg_{\psi} on the embedding; we explore further uses, such as operator learning,
in later sections.

On the other hand, because the decoder is what we would normally use to fit a
single object, it not only drives training by providing the loss signal, but also
lets the whole model inherit the decoder’s native capabilities, such as sampling
and density estimation in Flow Matching models. In this way the framework extends
object-specific operations, normally available on a per-object basis, to entire
families, while also producing representations suitable for downstream tasks.

Training data.

Our training data is a finite collection of sample sets
{𝒟1,𝒟2,…,𝒟N}\{\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{N}\}, where each 𝒟k={(si,wi)}i=12​n\mathcal{D}_{k}=\{(s_{i},w_{i})\}_{i=1}^{2n} is
a sample-based representation of an object 𝒪k\mathcal{O}_{k}, collected with the object
sampler 𝒮\mathcal{S}. We do not require this mapping to be unique: a single 𝒪k\mathcal{O}_{k}
may be described by several overlapping sample sets, possibly of varying size, and we
make no assumptions about their structure, such as a regular grid of points.
Working only with samples, rather than object-specific encodings, is precisely
what lets our framework support families of very different objects. The training
data can therefore be viewed as a dataset of datasets: each 𝒟k\mathcal{D}_{k}
describes a single object and is itself an ordinary dataset, though typically a
small one. Equivalently, a family is a distribution over datasets, and our model
learns to fit this distribution and to generalize across it.

Embedding network.

The embedder takes a sample set 𝒟\mathcal{D}
representing a member of the family and produces a fixed-size embedding vector
θ∈ℝm\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}. The network must be cardinality- and
permutation-invariant: its output must not depend on the size of the set or
on the order of its elements. DeepSets (Zaheer et al., 2017) and Set
Transformers (Lee et al., 2019) are examples of such architectures. The embedder
encodes each sample separately, possibly conditioned on the rest of the set, and pools the resulting codes by a (weighted) mean into a single vector. In
Section 3, we discuss a variation on this
architecture that better captures higher moments.

Training procedure.

In each training step we sample a batch of BB
sample sets {𝒟1,𝒟2,…,𝒟B}\{\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{B}\}, each of size
2​n2n. Every sample set 𝒟k\mathcal{D}_{k} is split into two halves of approximately
equal total weight. The first half 𝒟k,embed\mathcal{D}_{k,\text{embed}} is passed to
the embedder to produce an embedding θk=ℰϕ​(𝒟k,embed)\theta_{k}=\mathcal{E}_{\phi}(\mathcal{D}_{k,\text{embed}}), and the second half 𝒟k,train\mathcal{D}_{k,\text{train}} is scored by
the decoder gψg_{\psi} conditioned on θk\theta_{k}. This split discourages the
embedder from memorizing its input and forces θk\theta_{k} to capture
transferable structure of 𝒪k\mathcal{O}_{k}. The loss ℒ\mathcal{L} itself is
object-dependent, and we discuss several decoders and their loss functions in the
following sections. In every case, however, the training signal is backpropagated
through the decoder and the embedding θk\theta_{k} back to the embedder, so
that the two networks are trained jointly and the embedder learns to produce
embeddings the decoder can use. This procedure is illustrated in
Figure 1.

In the following three sections, we instantiate and evaluate our approach on concrete
families of distributions (Sec. 3), functions
(Sec. 4), and graphs (Sec. 5). The framework is
not limited to these: any higher-order object and family thereof fits within it, given the three
components introduced at the start of this section.

3 Distribution Embeddings

We first instantiate the framework on families of distributions. Every distribution
in a family shares a common sample space, and a single sample is one i.i.d. draw
from a member distribution.

Following Section 2, each distribution is represented as a
sample set {xi}i=1n\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}, and the embedder maps this set to a fixed-size
embedding θ\theta that parameterizes the decoder. For distributions, the decoder
gψg_{\psi} is a conditional generative model: it draws samples from the embedded
distribution and evaluates their density. Following the general training procedure,
each sample set is split in half: one half produces the embedding, the other scores
the decoder on held-out samples, and the embedder and decoder are trained jointly.

Figure 2: Distribution embeddings on the donut family. (a) Three donuts
(color-coded), each embedded from a small conditioning set of samples drawn from it
(crosses); the decoder then generates new samples (dots) from that embedding, recovering
the donut the conditioning points were drawn from. (b) The density the same
embedding induces over the plane, evaluated on a grid. From only a handful of conditioning
samples, the embedding captures each ring’s center, radius, and width.

The embedder is a Set Transformer with induced set attention blocks (ISABs)
(Lee et al., 2019). To help it capture higher moments of the distribution, we extend it
into multiple stages: each stage operates on the original samples combined with a
pooled summary of the previous stage’s output. Unlike stacking ISABs, as in the
original architecture, this multi-stage conditioning applies to any set encoder, not
only Set Transformers; we give pseudocode in Appendix D.
The embedding θ\theta parameterizes the decoder gψg_{\psi}, which can be any
generative model. Our experiments use both normalizing flows and flow matching. Each is trained
with its usual loss; see Appendix A for details on these models. Further
details and hyperparameters are in Appendix D.

4 Function Embeddings

We now turn to functions. A family of functions is a set that shares a common
structure, such as polynomials of bounded degree, or the solutions of a given
differential equation under varying initial conditions and parameters. A single
sample from a function ff is now an input–output pair (x,f⁡(x))(x,f(x)).

As described in Section 2, each object
is represented as a sample set {(xi,f⁡(xi))}i=1n\{(x_{i},f(x_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}, and the
embedder maps this set to a fixed-size embedding that parameterizes the decoder.
For functions, the decoder is a pointwise evaluator: given a query point xx in
the function’s domain, it produces an output f^​(x)\hat{f}(x) approximating f⁡(x)f(x).
Following the general training procedure, each sample set is split in half: one
half produces the embedding, the other scores the decoder on held-out samples.
The embedder and decoder are trained jointly to minimize the error between
f^​(x)\hat{f}(x) and f⁡(x)f(x).

In practice, the embedder can be the same multi-stage Set Transformer used in
Section 3, operating on samples formed by
concatenating xx, f⁡(x)f(x) (and optionally the pooled output of the previous
stage). While this already works well, we find it better to project xx, f⁡(x)f(x),
and the pooled output separately into a learned feature space and multiply the three
projections pointwise before passing the resulting set to the Set Transformer.
This three-way product incorporates the pooling information directly, rather than
through concatenation, and yields a stronger embedder; the results we report use
it, and we compare the two in Appendix E. The decoder
gψg_{\psi} is a single-head attention layer: it maps the query point xx to a query
vector and the embedding θ\theta to a set of synthetic key and value vectors,
then attends the query over them to predict f^​(x)\hat{f}(x). This performs better than a
plain feedforward network that concatenates xx and θ\theta.

The loss ℒ\mathcal{L} can be any regression loss, such as the mean squared error or the
Huber loss. All our experiments use the relative L2L^{2} error,
ℒ=𝔼f∼ℱ​[∥f−f^∥2/∥f∥2]\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{f\sim\mathcal{F}}\!\left[\lVert f-\hat{f}\rVert_{2}/\lVert f\rVert_{2}\right], as both training loss and evaluation metric, where the norms are
taken over the query points. Normalizing by ∥f∥2\lVert f\rVert_{2} makes the error
insensitive to the scale of the target, and it is standard in operator
learning (Lu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020). Further details, including
architectures and hyperparameters, are in Appendix E.

We train and evaluate function embeddings on three families: polynomials of
bounded degree, and the solutions of the damped harmonic oscillator and
Lotka–Volterra ordinary differential equations under varying initial
conditions and parameters. Descriptions and parameter ranges are in
Appendix E.

We further show that the learned embeddings can serve as inputs and outputs of
operator networks, which generalize ordinary functions to maps between
infinite-dimensional function spaces. Because our embeddings represent whole
functions as finite-dimensional vectors, an ordinary neural network becomes a
neural operator: it maps between compact vectors rather than predicting entire
functions, while the decoder expands each result into a function at arbitrary
query points. A single embedding also feeds many heads at once, so the decoder and
every operator share one representation; when this reuse is not needed and
operator learning is the only goal, the reconstruction phase can be skipped and
the operator trained directly. We train two such operators: for polynomials, one
mapping a function’s embedding to that of its derivative; for ODEs, one mapping
initial conditions and parameters to the embedding of the solution. Both are
simple feedforward networks; see Appendix E.

5 Graph Embeddings

Finally, we instantiate our framework on families of graphs. We consider
undirected labeled graphs 𝒢=(V,E)\mathcal{G}=(V,E) with no self-loops, where VV
is the vertex set and EE the edge set. A family is a collection of graphs that
share a common vertex set VV while the edge set EE varies. A single sample is
now an edge: an unordered pair of vertices.

Following the procedure laid out in Section 2, a graph is
represented solely as a set of randomly sampled edges {e1,…,en}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}. Each
edge is encoded as a two-hot vector, with ones at the positions of its two
vertices, so that the embedder is invariant to their order. The embedder maps this
set to a fixed-size embedding and uses the same multi-stage Set Transformer
architecture as in the previous sections. The embedding parameterizes a decoder
whose goal is to recover a Laplacian representation of the vertices: a
spectral representation of a graph that is a useful state representation for reinforcement learning
(Gomez et al., 2024). Following the general training procedure, the sampled edges
are split in half, one half producing the embedding and the other scoring the
decoder, and the two networks are trained jointly.

The decoder is trained with the ALLO objective (Appendix A.2). In
ALLO both the vertex-representation network and the lower-triangular matrix of
dual variables are fit to a single graph. We make both depend on the embedding
instead: the decoder maps each vertex to its representation vector conditioned on
θ\theta, and we exchange the fixed matrix of dual variables for a separate
neural network that predicts its entries from θ\theta. A single model thus
produces the Laplacian representation of any graph in the family. Further details
are in Appendix F.

We train and evaluate on three families: unweighted random trees, scale-free
networks, and weighted gridworlds. Detailed descriptions and parameter ranges are
in Appendix F.
The quality of learned representation and embeddings is assessed in two ways. First, by its geometry:
whether the distance between vertices effectively separates edges from non-edges. This is
meaningful because the Euclidean distance between two vertices in the Laplacian
representation is known to correlate with their temporal distance in the graph
(Gomez et al., 2024). Second, by reconstruction: whether a learned thresholding strategy can
recover adjacency, predicting an edge between two vertices when their
representation distance falls below a per-vertex threshold. We report two
thresholding strategies. The vertex-degrees strategy is an oracle that uses
each vertex’s true degree to set its threshold, whereas the medians network
is trained, solely from the sampled edges, to predict the median distance from a
vertex to its neighbors. It minimizes the mean absolute error between the
representation distances of sampled edges and the predicted medians:

ℒmed=𝔼(u,v)∈E​[|d⁡(u,v)−mu|+|d⁡(u,v)−mv|],\mathcal{L}_{\text{med}}=\mathbb{E}_{(u,v)\in E}\Big[\,\big|d(u,v)-m_{u}\big|+\big|d(u,v)-m_{v}\big|\,\Big],

where d⁡(u,v)=∥hu−hv∥2d(u,v)=\lVert h_{u}-h_{v}\rVert_{2} is the distance between the
representation vectors of uu and vv, and mum_{u} is the median distance
the network predicts for vertex uu to its neighbors.

6 Discussion

We demonstrated the generality and usefulness of our framework on three very distinct
higher-order objects: distributions, functions, and graphs. Appendix B
underscores how distinct these objects are, as each has historically been approached with
its own domain- and object-specific methods.

Distributions.

Distribution embeddings, as presented in Section 3, have already been applied to state tracking in partially observable domains as part of Neural Bayesian Filtering (Solinas et al., 2026b), which in turn underpins an online POMDP solver, Belief Embedding Tree Search (BETS) (Solinas et al., 2026a). Because the belief state is now a single fixed-size vector, policies and value functions can be learned directly on the embedding, which was not possible with the particle-based representations they replace. Compared to the closest prior work, our framework generalizes the Neural Statistician (Edwards and Storkey, 2016) by admitting any decoder, not just a variational autoencoder, and it goes beyond the continuous distributions to which Generative Distribution Embeddings (Fishman et al., 2025) have so far been applied.
BETS embeds discrete distributions by supplying a discrete generative model as the decoder, and we discuss discrete extensions of normalizing flows and flow matching in Appendix A.

Functions.

Similar to distribution embeddings, function embeddings reconstruct functions accurately from a handful of samples and even scale to higher-dimensional problems. They also demonstrate how useful a fixed-size vector representation of a higher-order object can be: unlike specialized approaches such as DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021) and FNO (Li et al., 2020), our embeddings let ordinary neural networks act as neural operators. Because these operators act on the learned embeddings rather than on raw function values, they carry none of the rigid input assumptions, such as regular grids, fixed sensor locations, or fixed resolutions, that these methods impose, yet they can still exploit that structure when it is available. In our experiments a single model maps parameters directly to ODE solutions, and solving a PDE requires nothing new: it reduces to mapping one embedding to another, the same operation we demonstrated with the differential operator that sends a function’s embedding to that of its derivative. Finally, because Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) (Raissi et al., 2019) only alter the training loss, our framework is open to them as well.

Graphs.

In Section 5 we took ALLO, an existing single-object model, and lifted it to a whole family for the first time. As originally proposed by Gomez et al. (2024), it operates on one graph at a time, so a new graph requires retraining from scratch. Using it as the object-specific decoder gψg_{\psi}, we made one small change: the fixed lower-triangular matrix of dual variables became a neural network that predicts its entries from the embedding. The lifted model learns vertex representations across a whole family of graphs, recovering a geometry that separates edges from non-edges well. Recovering the exact adjacency is harder, for two reasons: ALLO optimizes for spectral geometry and not reconstruction; and thresholding distances against a single per-vertex median cannot cleanly separate edges from non-edges at every vertex, particularly at high-degree hubs. Reconstruction-oriented approaches, such as those discussed in Appendix B, may be better suited to that task. Finally, because the embedding summarizes a graph as a single vector, it is a natural conditioning signal for reinforcement learning: an agent could act in an unknown environment, gather enough transitions to embed the local structure of its underlying Markov Decision Process, and condition its policy on that embedding.

7 Conclusion

We introduced the first framework general enough to learn compact
representations of higher-order objects and to generalize across families of
them. The framework accommodates any higher-order object through three
user-provided components: an object sampler, a parameterizable fitting model,
and a training loss. We instantiated it on three objects that have each
historically demanded vastly different architectures and training procedures:
distributions, functions, and graphs. In every case, a single trained model
represents an entire family and generalizes to members unseen during training.
Specific applications of this approach (notably distribution embeddings) have already been shown to be useful for tracking beliefs in POMDPs and in building cell dynamics models.

Appendix A Background

A.1 Generative Modeling

Normalizing flows (Papamakarios et al., 2021) model a
distribution with an invertible, differentiable map fψ:ℝd→ℝdf_{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d} that pushes a tractable base p0p_{0} (a standard Gaussian) to
the target. A sample is x=fψ​(z)x=f_{\psi}(z) with z∼p0z\sim p_{0}, and
its density follows from the change-of-variables formula

log⁡pψ​(x)=log⁡p0​(fψ−1​(x))+log⁡|det∂fψ−1∂x​(x)|.\log p_{\psi}(x)=\log p_{0}\!\left(f_{\psi}^{-1}(x)\right)+\log\left|\det\frac{\partial f_{\psi}^{-1}}{\partial x}(x)\right|.

Architectures such as RealNVP (Dinh et al., 2016) and neural spline flows
(Durkan et al., 2019) keep fψ−1f_{\psi}^{-1} and this log-determinant
tractable by composing layers with triangular Jacobians, and are trained by
maximizing the log-likelihood of the data. Flows live on continuous spaces; a
discrete support is reached through variational dequantization
(Ho et al., 2019), which adds learned noise to each point so the categorical
target becomes a continuous density, at the price of an implicit ordinal
structure over classes that may in fact be unordered.

Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022) is a continuous-time analogue
that replaces the composition of invertible layers with a learned velocity
field vψ​(z,τ)v_{\psi}(z,\tau) over τ∈[0,1]\tau\in[0,1]. Sampling integrates
z˙τ=vψ​(zτ,τ)\dot{z}_{\tau}=v_{\psi}(z_{\tau},\tau) from z0∼p0z_{0}\sim p_{0} and returns
x=defz1x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}z_{1}, and densities follow from integrating the instantaneous
change of variables along the same trajectory. Given the optimal-transport
interpolant zτ=(1−τ)​z+τ​xz_{\tau}=(1-\tau)\,z+\tau\,x between z∼p0z\sim p_{0} and
a target sample x∼px\sim p, training regresses onto the target velocity,

ℒFM​(ψ)=𝔼τ,z,x​‖vψ​(zτ,τ)−(x−z)‖2.\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau,z,x}\!\left\|v_{\psi}(z_{\tau},\tau)-(x-z)\right\|^{2}.

Dropping the closed-form-inverse requirement makes the transport more
expressive, at the cost of an ODE solve for both sampling and density. Its
categorical analogue, discrete flow matching (Gat et al., 2024),
interpolates between the data and a factorized noise distribution over classes
and trains a denoiser to recover the clean configuration, which is integrated
in reverse to sample; unlike dequantization it acts on the discrete support
directly, with no ordinal bias.

A.2 Augmented Lagrangian Laplacian Objective

An undirected graph without self-loops is fully described by its graph
Laplacian 𝐋=𝐃−𝐀\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{A}, where 𝐃\mathbf{D} is the
diagonal degree matrix and 𝐀\mathbf{A} the (weighted) adjacency matrix. The
Laplacian representation assigns each vertex ii the dd-dimensional
vector formed from the ii-th component of the dd smallest eigenvectors of
𝐋\mathbf{L}. Distances in this representation encode temporal structure of the
underlying state space, making it a useful state representation for
reinforcement learning (Gomez et al., 2024).

The augmented Lagrangian Laplacian objective (ALLO) (Gomez et al., 2024)
learns this representation for a single graph from its sampled edges. It is a
saddle-point objective that recovers the true eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
𝐋\mathbf{L} with little hyperparameter tuning, jointly optimizing a network
that maps each vertex to its representation vector against an explicit
lower-triangular matrix of dual variables (βj​k)1≤k≤j≤d(\beta_{jk})_{1\leq k\leq j\leq d}, one per pair of eigenvectors. The diagonal entries βj​j\beta_{jj} converge
to values proportional to the eigenvalues, and the off-diagonal entries
βj​k\beta_{jk} enforce orthogonality between the eigenvectors.

Appendix B Related Work

To the best of our knowledge, no prior approach represents and learns from
families of general higher-order objects. Existing work targets specific object
types: mostly functions, through operator learning, and more recently
distributions across a range of domains.

Distributions.

Kernel mean embeddings (KMEs) (Muandet et al., 2017)
are a classical non-parametric way to represent a distribution as a point in a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space, but they scale poorly with dimension, are not
natively generative, and require a kernel whose choice and hyperparameters
usually demand domain knowledge. The Neural Statistician
(Edwards and Storkey, 2016) instead learns distribution embeddings from
i.i.d. samples and uses them to condition a variational autoencoder (VAE).
Generative Distribution Embeddings (GDEs) (Fishman et al., 2025) generalize both by
allowing an arbitrary conditional generative model as the decoder; they also
justify distributional invariance theoretically and show that latent distances
correlate with the Wasserstein-2 distance, though they have been applied only
to continuous distributions in computational biology. At the same time,
Solinas et al. (2026b) independently proposed distribution embeddings to
represent belief states in a filtering algorithm for partially observable state
tracking, outperforming traditional filters.

Functions.

Neural Processes (NPs)
(Garnelo et al., 2018) combine Gaussian processes and neural
networks to learn distributions over functions: they encode a set of
input-output pairs into a distribution over latent codes, which a decoder
evaluates at a query input to predict the function’s value there, and are
trained by maximizing an ELBO. DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021) and FNO
(Li et al., 2020) are neural operators that learn maps between function spaces,
typically PDE solution operators, and so act on families of related functions;
DeepONet uses branch and trunk networks, while FNO learns the operator in the
spectral domain via Fourier transforms. Both carry function-specific biases:
DeepONet needs conditioning points on a predefined grid and FNO assumes uniform
grids. Later variants such as GeoFNO (Li et al., 2023) relax some of these
but remain tied to functions (see Kovachki et al. (2023) for an
overview). Finally, function encoders (Ingebrand et al., 2024)
represent a function as a linear combination of learned basis functions, first
for reinforcement learning and later for transfer learning
(Ingebrand et al., 2025).

Graphs.

Most graph representation learning embeds nodes rather than
whole graphs: GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017), for example, learns an inductive
node encoder that generalizes to unseen nodes and graphs, but yields one vector per node
rather than a single representation of the graph. graph2vec (Narayanan et al., 2017)
instead embeds an entire graph as a fixed-size vector, learned unsupervised by treating
rooted subgraphs as words in the manner of document embeddings, though the resulting
representation is task-agnostic and tied to graph-specific substructures. A separate line
of work learns to generate graphs: GraphVAE (Simonovsky and Komodakis, 2018) decodes a
graph from a latent code with a variational autoencoder, GraphRNN (You et al., 2018)
builds graphs autoregressively, and DiGress (Vignac et al., 2022) does so through
discrete denoising diffusion. These models capture a distribution over graphs but do not
expose a reusable embedding of any given graph.

Compared to the approaches above, our framework is not tied to a single object type and
bakes in no domain-specific assumptions, though such biases can still be reintroduced
through the decoder gψg_{\psi} and the loss ℒ\mathcal{L} when useful. This generality is what
lets many of these methods be recast as special cases of our framework under particular
choices of gψg_{\psi} and ℒ\mathcal{L}, and it lets any existing single-object model serve as
a decoder, making the framework straightforward to adapt to new problems and domains.

Appendix D Distribution Embeddings

D.1 Distribution Families

Donuts.

Two-dimensional ring-shaped distributions, each fully described
by a center c∈ℝ2c\in\mathbb{R}^{2}, a radius rr, and a width ww. A sample is drawn as
x=c+r​a​(1+w​b)x=c+r\,a\,(1+w\,b), where aa is a uniformly random unit vector and
b∼𝒰⁡[−12,12]b\sim\mathcal{U}[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}], so the points fill an annulus of
mean radius rr whose thickness is r​wrw. The family
draws the center from 𝒩⁡(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0,I), the radius uniformly from [0.5,2][0.5,2], and
the width uniformly from [0.01,0.75][0.01,0.75], spanning both thin and thick donuts.

D.2 Embedding Properties

The main text shows that the embedding space is low-dimensional and aligned with the
donut’s generative parameters. Two further experiments test whether it is also
linear: whether straight lines and vector arithmetic between embeddings
correspond to meaningful operations on the distributions. Both use the same flow-matching
model from Section 3 with 512512-sample embeddings, and every panel below shows the learned density
decoded from a single embedding.

Interpolation.

Figure 5 embeds two donuts that
differ in center, radius, and width, and decodes the densities along the segment
zα=(1−α)​z0+α​z1z_{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)\,z_{0}+\alpha\,z_{1} between their embeddings. During
training, every embedding the decoder sees is produced by the encoder from the samples of
some donut; the interior points zαz_{\alpha} are not, being mere convex combinations of two
such embeddings, so nothing forces the decoder to treat them sensibly. Yet each decodes to
a single well-formed ring, and the rings translate, grow, and thin as α\alpha increases.
This is neither of the two naive interpolations. Mixing the two densities,
(1−α)​p0+α​p1(1-\alpha)\,p_{0}+\alpha\,p_{1}, cross-fades one ring into the other and never
produces an intermediate donut, and moving paired samples along straight lines smears the
rings into a cloud that is no donut at all. The embedding instead moves through the
family: every intermediate point is a donut with intermediate center, radius, and width.

Embedding arithmetic.

Starting from a base donut AA, we raise one
property to obtain BB or CC, and both to obtain DD. If the properties are encoded
along independent directions, the parallelogram identity zD≈zB+zC−zAz_{D}\approx z_{B}+z_{C}-z_{A}
holds, and decoding its right-hand side predicts DD without ever seeing samples of
it. Figures 6 and 7
show this for two positional properties and for a positional and a shape property.
The predictions agree with the true corner to a cosine similarity above 0.9990.999, with
relative errors of 3.1%3.1\% and 4.2%4.2\% of ‖zD‖\|z_{D}\|, at the 3.9%3.9\% floor set by
sampling noise in a 512512-sample embedding. The identity extends to three properties as
zE≈zB+zC+zD−2​zAz_{E}\approx z_{B}+z_{C}+z_{D}-2z_{A}, and it breaks only where the family itself is
not additive: a donut’s absolute thickness is the product of its radius and its relative
width, so raising both interacts multiplicatively, and no additive code can represent a
product. The triple in Figure 8, which raises radius
and width together, misses by 24%24\% and decodes to a visibly thinner ring, whereas
triples avoiding that pair stay near their 5.6%5.6\% noise floor.

D.4 Hyperparameters

The embedder is the multi-stage set encoder of Algorithm 1 with an
ISAB at every stage and weighted-mean pooling; the pooled output of the last stage is
the embedding θ\theta. Both generator heads are conditioned on θ\theta by
concatenation: every MLP inside a head receives θ\theta appended to its input.
Table 7 lists the hyperparameters of both heads,
together with the evaluation protocol.

Flow-matching head.

An MLP velocity field v⁡(x,t,θ)v(x,t;\theta) that
reads xx concatenated with a sinusoidal embedding of tt. It is trained with the
conditional flow-matching loss on the linear path
xt=(1−(1−σmin)​t)​z+t​xx_{t}=(1-(1-\sigma_{\min})\,t)\,z+t\,x from Gaussian noise zz to a sample
xx, regressing the target velocity x−(1−σmin)​zx-(1-\sigma_{\min})\,z. Sampling integrates
the learned ODE from noise to data with the midpoint rule, and the density follows
from the continuous change-of-variables formula, accumulating the divergence of vv
along the reverse path.

Normalizing-flow head.

A stack of masked coupling layers on a standard
normal base, each of which updates one coordinate given the other and then the
reverse, using the non-linear squared (NLSq) transform of Ziegler and Rush (2019),
f⁡(x)=a+b​x+c/(1+(d​x+e)2)f(x)=a+bx+c/(1+(dx+e)^{2}), whose five parameters are predicted by a
conditioned MLP. It is trained by maximum likelihood, and both sampling and density
evaluation take a single pass through the stack.

Evaluation.

Each held-out donut is embedded from nn of its samples. The
NLL is scored on fresh samples of the same donut, and a set of generated samples is
compared with the true donut by the sliced Wasserstein distance and by the recovery
error of its center, radius, and width, which are estimated from the generated
samples by the method of moments. The KDE and parametric fit baselines are fit to the same
nn samples.

Appendix E Function Embeddings

E.1 Families

Polynomials.

Univariate polynomials
f⁡(x)=∑k=0dck​xkf(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}x^{k} of degree dd up to three, with coefficients drawn
uniformly and evaluated on a bounded interval. See Table 8 for concrete ranges. Unlike the oscillatory ODE solutions
below, this family spans a wide range of output scales, from near-zero constants to
cubics reaching into the hundreds. We train the embedding over degrees 00–33; the
differential operator is then trained over degrees 11–33 only, since the derivative
of a degree-00 polynomial is identically zero and undefined under a relative-L2L^{2}
loss.

Damped harmonic oscillator.

A chain of MM unit masses on
a line, each tied to its rest position by an on-site spring of frequency ω\omega and
to its neighbors by coupling springs, under uniform velocity damping γ\gamma. For a
single mass this reduces to x¨+2​γ​x˙+ω2​x=0\ddot{x}+2\gamma\dot{x}+\omega^{2}x=0, whose
closed-form solution covers the under-, over-, and critically-damped regimes.
Stacking the MM positions and velocities into a state zz gives a linear system
z˙=A​z\dot{z}=Az with the closed-form solution z⁡(t)=exp⁡(A​t)​z0z(t)=\exp(At)\,z_{0}, so each
trajectory is a 2​M2M-dimensional state evolving in time. Varying
M∈{1,2,4,8,16}M\in\{1,2,4,8,16\} sweeps the state dimension from 22 to 3232, the axis of
the dimension-scaling result in Table 3.

Lotka–Volterra.

The predator–prey system x˙=α​x−β​x​y\dot{x}=\alpha x-\beta xy, y˙=δ​x​y−γ​y\dot{y}=\delta xy-\gamma y, with a two-dimensional state
(x,y)(x,y) tracking the prey and predator populations. It has no elementary
closed-form solution, so each trajectory is integrated numerically with a
Dormand–Prince solver implemented in diffrax (Kidger, 2021). A function is
specified by its initial state (x0,y0)(x_{0},y_{0}) and the four interaction rates
(α,β,γ,δ)(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta), and the resulting trajectories are periodic
orbits of varying amplitude.

E.2 Architectures & Hyperparameters

The embedder is the multi-stage set encoder of
Appendix C (Algorithm 1), whose
stages differ only in how each sample is combined with the running set-level
summary cc. We use two such stage blocks, both built on the induced set attention
block (ISAB) of the Set Transformer (Lee et al., 2019). The two stage-block variants,
along with the decoder, operator, and training recipe, share a single set of
hyperparameters, listed in Table 9.

Concatenation embedder.

Each stage concatenates the per-sample input
xx, output f⁡(x)f(x), and (from the second stage on) the pooled summary cc into a
single vector, and passes the resulting set through an ISAB. This is the direct
analogue of the encoder used for distributions, with cc appended to every sample.

Dot-product embedder.

Each stage instead projects xx, f⁡(x)f(x), and cc
through separate MLPs and multiplies the three projections elementwise before
passing the product to the ISAB. The elementwise product lets the summary modulate
the per-sample features multiplicatively rather than enter as an extra input
channel, and we found it to yield a stronger encoder; it is the default in all
reported results. The two variants are compared in
Tables 11 and 10.

Decoder.

The decoder reconstructs ff at a query point xx by
single-head cross-attention. An MLP maps xx to a query vector, while two linear
projections expand the embedding θ\theta into a small fixed set of synthetic
key and value vectors; the query attends over the keys and returns the resulting
convex combination of values as f^​(x)\hat{f}(x). Treating the embedding as a short
sequence of learned slots outperforms a plain MLP that concatenates xx and
θ\theta directly.

Operator networks.

Both operators are plain feedforward MLPs acting
directly in vector spaces. The differential operator maps a function’s embedding to
the embedding of its derivative and is trained as a second stage on the frozen
embedding; the ODE solution operator maps a raw vector of initial conditions and
parameters to the embedding of the corresponding solution. In both cases the
decoder above expands the predicted embedding back into a function at arbitrary
query points.

Appendix F Graph Embeddings

F.1 Families

Random trees.

Uniformly random labeled trees on nn vertices, drawn
through Prüfer sequences: a uniformly random length-(n−2)(n-2) string over the nn
vertex labels decodes to a distinct labeled tree, so every labeled tree is equally
likely. The graphs are unweighted, and we evaluate them at n∈{10,20}n\in\{10,20\}.

Scale-free networks.

Barabási–Albert graphs (Albert and Barabási, 2002) grown by preferential
attachment: vertices are added one at a time, and each new vertex attaches to
m=2m=2 earlier vertices chosen with probability proportional to their current
degree. This yields a heavy-tailed degree distribution with a few high-degree hubs,
which are the vertices reconstruction struggles with most. The graphs are
unweighted, again at n∈{10,20}n\in\{10,20\}.

Gridworlds.

The only weighted family. Each graph starts from a fixed
4×44\times 4 grid (n=16n=16) and removes up to three of its edges
uniformly at random, subject to the remaining graph staying connected. Every
surviving edge carries a positive weight drawn uniformly, and edges are sampled with
frequency proportional to their weight, so the family tests reconstruction of a
weighted adjacency rather than a binary one.

F.2 Architectures & Hyperparameters

The encoder is the multi-stage set encoder of Appendix C,
built on ISABs and applied to the sampled edges, each given as the pair of one-hot
labels of its endpoints. Table 12 lists the hyperparameters;
across families only the number of representation dimensions and the number of edges
sampled per graph change. Each sampled edge set is split in half between producing
the embedding and scoring the decoder, and every configuration is trained with three
seeds.

Decoder.

The decoder is the ALLO objective of Section A.2
with both of its trainable parts conditioned on the embedding. An MLP maps a vertex’s
one-hot label, concatenated with θ\theta, to its dd-dimensional representation,
and a second MLP predicts each dual variable βj​k\beta_{jk} from θ\theta and the
index pair (j,k)(j,k). The diagonal duals provide the eigenvalue estimates; the
representation used downstream drops the constant eigenvector and rescales every
remaining coordinate by the inverse square root of its eigenvalue. The augmented
Lagrangian carries the penalty coefficient bb and an L2L_{2} regularizer on the dual
variables, and its saddle point is optimized with optimistic Adam.

Reconstruction heads.

The medians network is an MLP that maps a vertex’s
one-hot label, concatenated with θ\theta, through a softplus to mum_{u}, the
predicted median distance from vertex uu to its neighbors. It is trained with the
mean absolute error discussed in Section 5 and predicts an edge between
uu and vv whenever d⁡(u,v)≤mu+mvd(u,v)\leq m_{u}+m_{v}. The degree oracle instead connects
each vertex to its deg⁡(u)\deg(u) nearest vertices, thresholding halfway between the
deg⁡(u)\deg(u)-th and the next nearest distance, with the threshold of a pair taken as the
mean of its endpoints’ thresholds. For the weighted gridworld family, the transition
network is an MLP that scores every ordered vertex pair from its representation
distance and θ\theta; a global softmax over all pairs, excluding self-loops,
turns the scores into a normalized symmetric adjacency, trained by the negative
log-likelihood of the held-out sampled edges. Since edges are sampled with frequency
proportional to their weight, this recovers the true adjacency up to its overall
scale, which frequency sampling cannot identify.

Evaluation.

Each held-out graph is embedded from its sampled edges, and
the geometry is scored by ranking every vertex pair by representation distance: we
report the ROC-AUC and the average precision (AP) of the resulting edge detector.
Reconstruction is scored by the precision, recall, and F1 of the predicted adjacency
against the true one, with counts pooled over all held-out graphs, for both the degree
oracle and the medians network. On gridworld, the transition head’s adjacency is
compared with the true normalized adjacency by Spearman rank correlation, over the
true edges and over all vertex pairs (rescaled by the ceiling that the tied non-edges
impose), and by the mean absolute error of the off-diagonal weights.

Table 13 extends Table 4 with the average
precision of the geometry, the full precision and recall of both reconstruction heads,
and the weighted gridworld family. The medians network reconstructs with a recall near
one but a low precision on the n=20n=20 families, the signature of the over-prediction
